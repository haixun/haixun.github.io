This is not a prediction problem.

It is a design–build–test–learn loop where the cycle time is weeks, the batch size is tiny, and the measurements are noisy. The machine learning that transfers is active learning, Bayesian optimisation and experimental design under a brutal budget — not supervised learning at scale.

One number explains the whole field. There are about 150 million protein sequences in UniProt and about 263,000 experimentally determined structures in the PDB. Function labels are scarcer still, and almost every one is specific to the assay that produced it. You will never have enough labels. Everything below follows from that.

The other thing to internalise early: you do not need a biology degree. You need a different, smaller, stranger subset of biology than a bench scientist carries. As one computational scientist embedded in a protein lab put it, the biology a computational biologist needs to know is not necessarily the same as an experimental biologist needs to know.

Company claims of 15–35% at twenty designs per target exist, and none has been peer-reviewed or independently reproduced.

What people from your background get wrong

Treating it as Kaggle

Benchmarks are contaminated and labels contradict each other. The same molecule appears in standard sets labelled both ways for the same property. Reported accuracy partly measures how well you fitted the contradiction.

Optimising affinity

Your design has to express, stay soluble, and survive formulation. Aggregation, viscosity and immunogenicity kill more candidates than weak binding does, and almost no model addresses them.

Assuming mutations add up

Epistasis is the norm. The landscape is rugged, so linear intuitions mislead and a double mutant is routinely worse than either single.

Trusting the assay

Two measurements of the same compound against the same target in the main public database agree at about R² 0.3. A model that reproduced that database perfectly would score about 0.3. Published models score about 0.3.

Ignoring synthesis cost

Nearly every ML-guided directed evolution method proposes libraries nobody can afford to build. Cost is a first-class constraint, not an afterthought.

Chasing optimal

Directed evolution wants a sufficient protein under constraints, not an optimal one. The goals mismatch is the single most-cited reason this literature has underdelivered.

The asymmetry nobody mentions

There is no published first-person account of a wet-lab biologist who learned to build models. Every substantial crossing-over narrative runs in your direction. That is an advantage — the path is worn and people will help you walk it — and it is also a quiet warning about who ends up holding the judgement in these partnerships.

The best-documented successful pairing in the literature describes itself not as complementary skills but as deliberately engineered conflict: arranging circumstances where a data scientist and a chemist must disagree and work it out. Plan for that rather than for a clean division of labour.
