Evidence file

Seed-stage founder credentials in AI for drug discovery

An evidence file, not an essay. Compiled 11 November 2026 for the ai4bio exploration. It asks a single question — who actually gets funded to start an AI-for-drug company, and what were the seed investors buying? — and answers it as a taxonomy of founder archetypes with named cases. Method: six parallel research passes over primary sources, preferring SEC filings (S-1s, 8-Ks, Form 3s, a Form D), company team and about pages, university technology-transfer and conflict-of-interest disclosures, clinicaltrials.gov's API, Europe PMC, and venture-firm writing, with trade press as corroboration. Every role is labelled by the kind of source it came from, because the central finding of this file is that the word "co-founder" is applied inconsistently and often self-reported. Limits: the session's shared web-search budget was exhausted early, so roughly two thirds of the evidence below came from direct fetches of known URLs rather than discovery searches; GeekWire, Fierce, Endpoints, Businesswire and several firm sites returned 403. Named gaps are listed in "Gaps and conflicts" rather than filled. This file completes the founder census that "The Pairing" flagged as an unfinished next task.

The short version

  1. The modal funded founder in this space is not the famous professor and not an outsider. It is a recent trainee from a famous lab, paired with an operator the investors bring. The only quantitative base available — a study of all 60 biotechs that IPO'd in the first half of 2021 — found that 63% of founders held a PhD, that "most founders were principal investigators or recent graduates from the lab where the technology was developed," and that only 3% of CEOs came from academia, against 39% from pharma and 29% from biotech (Huayamares et al., arXiv 2205.00993). The founder supplies the science; someone else is hired to run the company.
  2. "Co-founder" is not a reliable fact and should never be taken at face value. David Baker's own conflict-of-interest page claims founder roles in 21 companies; several of those companies' own materials give him a different title or none. Monod Bio's seed press release calls him "Scientific Advisor" in the founders paragraph and "Co-founder of Monod Bio" in his quote byline — inside one document. Outpace Bio lists him as a "Collaborator" and is absent from his own list. Xaira appears in both the founder and the non-founder-advisor tables on his page. Patent co-inventorship is the stable fact; "co-founder" is a label applied retroactively and asymmetrically (ipd.uw.edu/baker-technology-transfer-roles).
  3. A postdoc is sufficient; the professor is frequently not a founder at all. Clean, sourced cases of trainees founding alone with the professor explicitly not a founder: Outpace Bio (Marc Lajoie, Scott Boyken — Baker postdocs; Baker a "Collaborator"), AI Proteins (Chris Bahl — Baker postdoc; Baker not involved at all), A-Alpha Bio (two UW grad students; UW CoMotion names only them), Tome Biosciences (Abudayyeh and Gootenberg; Feng Zhang given no role by a16z or BioSpace), Integrated Biosciences (Felix Wong, the Collins-lab postdoc who was lead author on the Nature ML-antibiotics paper, as CEO). Harvard's own technology-development office named the shift: "lab members are stepping up to take the role of CEO, rather than chief scientific officer or chief technology officer" (Nature Biotechnology, 2019).
  4. The professor's name does not by itself produce a large round. A venture firm deciding to build a company around the lab does. Within the single Baker lineage the spread is roughly 40x to 1,000x: trainee-led, licensed-out-of-the-university companies raised $0.85M–$25M from Seattle-regional seed capital (Cyrus $850K, A-Alpha $2.8M, Archon $20M, Monod $25M, Skape ~$9M), while firm-incubated companies with the same scientific lineage raised $50M–$1bn+ (Vilya $50M→$71M, ARCH as "Founder and lead investor"; Xaira >$1bn, jointly incubated by ARCH and Foresite Labs).
  5. A frontier-AI-lab pedigree does substitute for a biology credential at seed — but only a specific pedigree, and only on one side of a sharp boundary. EvolutionaryScale (ex-Meta ESM team, no biology PhD, no CSO, no SAB) raised a $142M seed; Chai Discovery (TechCrunch, verbatim: "No biology training, degrees, or wet-lab credentials are mentioned for any founder") raised ~$30M at $150M and is now at $3.8bn; Latent Labs' sole founder has a computer-vision PhD and raised $50M. But Cradle's founders are also non-biologists — from Google product rather than DeepMind research — and raised a €5.5M seed, about a twenty-fifth of EvolutionaryScale's. The thing being priced is the AlphaFold / ESM / ChatGPT line on a CV, not "being a technologist."
  6. The boundary is between selling to drug developers and being one. Every pure AI-lab-diaspora company in the census sells tools or models and owns no drug: Latent Labs ("won't develop its own therapeutic candidates"), EvolutionaryScale, Chai, Cradle, Bioptimus, Profluent ("does not plan to develop treatments itself"), Boltz, Tamarind. Every company in the census with its own clinical pipeline has biology or clinical credentials at or adjacent to the founder level, or bought the asset outright.
  7. Credential-free founding is routine in tools and essentially does not happen in therapeutics without a borrowed credential. The therapeutics cases that look like exceptions all turn out to have rented the credential: Charm Therapeutics is Laksh Aithani (no PhD found; ML engineering at Exscientia plus a YC-backed NLP startup) plus David Baker as the other of only two named co-founders; Cellular Intelligence is a software founder with a $575M exit plus four National-Academy-class scientific co-founders; GloGlo is two consumer-AI founders plus a tenured UCSF professor as co-founder. The one near-clean exception, Reverie Labs, earned its way across the boundary by selling software to pharma first and only then building a pipeline.
  8. A PhD is not mandatory. A credential is. Named founders with no stated PhD who raised real money: Sean McClain (Absci — a BSc, solo founder, still CEO at IPO, 11.28% pre-IPO, the largest individual holding), Ankit Gupta (Reverie Labs — Harvard AB/SM in CS, built an oncology pipeline, sold to Ginkgo), Laksh Aithani (Charm), Deniz Kavi and Sherry Liu (Tamarind), Gabriele Corso (Boltz — left his MIT PhD to be CEO).
  9. Venture-creation firms do not back founders; they are the founder, and the academic's economics are documentably small. Generate:Biomedicines' S-1 states that "Flagship Pioneering founded Generate in 2018 as Flagship VL56, Inc." At IPO the tenured Dartmouth professor who is named co-founder and sitting CTO held under 1%, against Flagship's 56.6% and a recruited ex-Merck CEO's 6.4%. In Moderna's S-1 the two academic co-founders who were not directors do not appear in the ownership table at all. In Relay's S-1 the recruited operator CEO holds 2.31%, the ex-Vertex-CTO co-founder 1.56%, and the two academic founders are absent.
  10. No AI-designed or AI-discovered drug has been approved anywhere, and the two most advanced are only now in Phase 3. Registry-verified: Insilico's rentosertib began a 320-patient Phase 3 on 9 September 2026 (NCT07687459, primary completion 2029); Generate's GB-0895 is in two 786-patient Phase 3s in asthma. Isomorphic Labs has zero registered clinical trials. Xaira has zero. The most recent peer-reviewed assessment, a 16-author Nature Reviews Drug Discovery review from November 2026, says the evidence of clinical impact is "disappointingly limited."

Three concrete traps, all from primary documents:

  • Michael Bronstein is not a founder of Relation Therapeutics. He is an SAB member. Relation's founders, per DCVC's own release, are Charles Campbell Roberts (co-founder and chair), David Roblin (CEO), Jake Taylor-King and Lindsay Edwards. Bronstein's actual company record is Invision (acquired by Intel) and Fabula AI (acquired by Twitter) — computer vision and social-graph AI, not biotech. This is the cleanest mislabelling case found.
  • Feng Zhang is not a founder of Tome Biosciences. a16z's own announcement names only Omar Abudayyeh and Jonathan Gootenberg, who "trained under CRISPR pioneer Feng Zhang"; BioSpace's launch piece does not mention Zhang at all. Tome raised $213M and laid off 131 people — nearly its entire staff — about eight months after launch.
  • George Church's own disclosure page separates founder from adviser, and the separation is large. Roughly 55 entries are marked "F" for founder (of which about nine are non-profits or projects, so ~45–46 companies), against a separate list of ~43 past advisory roles, a licensing list, a consulting list of ~23 pharma and tech companies, and a list of ~60 funds that have invested in his lab's newcos (arep.med.harvard.edu/gmc/tech.html). Published counts of "his" companies vary — Wikipedia says ~50, GEN says 38 — because they are counting different columns.

Practical rule for this group: treat a self-disclosure page as a claim, a company team page as a claim, and an SEC filing as a fact. Of all the Baker companies, exactly one — Icosavax — has a regulated document calling him a co-founder: "Our scientific co-founders, Neil King and David Baker, are world leaders in protein design" (S-1, 7 July 2021).

Archetype 1 — The academic-star spinout

1a. David Baker and the Institute for Protein Design

Two authoritative primary lists exist and they do not agree with each other, which is itself the finding.

The institute's list (ipd.uw.edu/research/translation) names 11 spinouts: Cyrus Biotechnology (2014), PvP Biologics (2016, acquired by Takeda 2020), Icosavax (2017, acquired by AstraZeneca 2024), A-Alpha Bio (2018), Neoleukin Therapeutics (2018, merged into Neurogene 2023), Mopac Biologics (2020), Monod Bio (2021), Lila Biologics (2022), Vilya (2022), Archon Biosciences (2023), Skape Bio ApS (2025). Headline figures: "$1B+ raised by alumni," "largest acquisition: $1.1B." The more recently updated About page says "more than ten spinout companies that together have raised over $2 billion." The institute states plainly that "as a non-commercial entity, the IPD receives minimal or no direct benefit from these commercial activities."

Baker's own list (ipd.uw.edu/baker-technology-transfer-roles, header "Updated April 2024") claims founder, co-founder or scientific co-founder roles in 21 companies: Prospect Genomics (1999), Bio Architecture Lab (2008), Arzeda (2009), Pregenen (2011), Cyrus Biotech (2014*), PvP Biologics (2016*), Virvio (2017), Icosavax (2018*), A-Alpha Bio (2018*), Neoleukin (2018*), Sana Biotechnology (2019), Lyell Immunotherapeutics (2019), Mopac Biologics (2020*), Monod Bio (2021*), Brahma (2021), Charm (2021), Axxis (2022*), Lila (2022*), Vilya (2023*), Archon Biosciences (2023*), Xaira Therapeutics (2024). The asterisk means "emerged from the Institute for Protein Design Translational Investigator Program." A separate "Advisor (Non-Founder)" table holds two entries: Cue Biopharma (2014) and — again — Xaira Therapeutics (2024).

Where the two lists and the companies disagree:

  • Lyell Immunopharma's own leadership page lists exactly three founders — Rick Klausner, Crystal Mackall, Stan Riddell — and does not mention Baker. Outpace Bio's story page, separately, says "Baker was among the co-founders of Lyell Immunopharma in 2018, along with Lajoie, Boyken, and Riddell." Three sources, three accounts.
  • Sana Biotechnology's team page lists no founders at all and does not mention Baker. No Sana S-1 mentions "Institute for Protein Design" or "David Baker."
  • A-Alpha Bio: UW CoMotion's startup record names only David Younger and Randolph Lopez, both then UW graduate students whose AlphaSeq platform came out of PhD work co-advised by Baker and Eric Klavins. Madrona's investment memo calls Baker their thesis advisor and gives him no company role. Baker's page counts it as a founder role.
  • Archon Biosciences: the company's own launch release says Baker "co-invented the Archon platform alongside co-founders" and never calls him a founder; only James Lazarovits (CEO) and George Ueda (CTO), both former IPD Translational Investigators, are co-founders.
  • Outpace Bio lists Baker as a "Collaborator" and is absent from his own 21-company list.
  • AI Proteins — founded by Chris Bahl, who "pioneered the ability to computationally design miniproteins de novo as a postdoctoral fellow with Nobel Laureate David Baker" — gives Baker no role at all and appears on neither list. A Baker-trained scientist building a direct competitor with no Baker equity or title.
  • Axxis (2022*) carries the IPD-program asterisk on Baker's page but is absent from the institute's own 11-company roster; axxis.bio is a parked domain. Unexplained.
  • Brahma (2021) — one line on Baker's page and nothing else found anywhere. Terrain Bio appears on neither list and could not be verified to exist; do not include it.

The institute's translational machinery is the most transferable part of the story, and it is designed to manufacture founders out of trainees. The Translational Investigator Program (launched 2014, seeded with a $1.4M Life Sciences Discovery Fund award plus $5.2M in matching commitments) runs four phases — Exploration, Translation, Incubation, Impact. The institute's own language: investigators are encouraged to "step into the role of founder"; in the Incubation phase "Investigators take on a founder role. Projects stay at UW while markets are analyzed and licensing agreements are drafted." Before any venture money, projects are de-risked with non-dilutive translational grants: Washington Research Foundation technology-commercialization grants in escalating phases (Stephanie Berger, → Mopac: $300,500 then $700,000; Lazarovits and Ueda, → Archon: $143,959 then $498,804, with ~$7M in total grant funding reported for the AbC platform), the NIH-funded WE-REACH hub at UW, and the institute's own philanthropic donors — Icosavax's CEO states in the launch release that "we started Icosavax with seed funding from its philanthropic supporters." UW CoMotion negotiates the licences, holds the patent portfolios, and runs a physical incubator (PvP, A-Alpha and Monod were all Fluke Hall tenants).

What the university takes, from the one fully documented case (Icosavax S-1, UW 2018 Agreement): 1,120,771 common shares issued to UW across December 2017 and August 2018, fully vested on issuance; an annual maintenance fee in the "mid four figures"; development and regulatory milestones up to $350,000 per candidate across six candidates plus $1M commercial milestones; a "fixed low single digit percentage royalty" on net sales; and a sublicensing revenue share of "mid-single digit to mid-double digit percentage." Total cash paid to UW through June 2021: about $290,000. The money is in the equity, not the fees. Also disclosed and material to anyone building on federally funded protein design: the US government retains a paid-up non-exclusive licence and march-in rights "exercisable if public health crises so demand"; HHMI holds a research-use licence because Baker is an HHMI investigator; and the Gates Foundation holds global-access commitments on three of six pathogens.

Did the October 2024 Nobel change the pattern? On this evidence, no — it changed the marketing.

  • The largest event in the lineage, Xaira's >$1bn launch, was 23 April 2024, six months before the prize. Any account in which the Nobel unlocked mega-rounds is wrong on the dates.
  • Archon emerged from stealth three weeks after the prize with a $20M seed — but it was incorporated in early 2023 and the round was already assembled. What changed was the headline: "Archon Biosciences Emerges from Stealth to Advance a New Class of Biologic Invented in Nobel Prize-Winning Computational Protein Design Lab."
  • Formation rate is flat to down. 2021–2023 produced seven Baker-co-founded companies (Monod, Brahma, Charm, Axxis, Lila, Vilya, Archon). The ~2.5 years from April 2024 to November 2026 produced two that can be documented (Xaira, Skape).
  • The only post-Nobel spinout on the institute's roster, Skape Bio, raised about $9M — at the small end of the historical distribution, below Monod's $25M (2022) and Archon's $20M (2024).
  • The institute's 2025–2026 news output contains zero spinout or funding announcements — it is RFantibody, RFdiffusion2, RFdiffusion3, a vaccine trial, a hire, and an NVIDIA compute partnership. The post-Nobel institutional emphasis is open model releases, not company formation.
  • Baker's disclosure page has not been updated since April 2024 and his faculty bio, which carries 2026 honours, still says "co-founded 21 biotechnology companies" — even though Skape Bio's own site names him a co-founder. The count is stale, not static.

1b. The other lab lineages

Carolyn Bertozzi (Stanford). Her role at Lycia Therapeutics is pinned down by two primary sources and the company's own page, and it is "academic founder" plus chair of the Scientific Advisory Board — not officer, not director. Eli Lilly's Form 8-K of 23 August 2021 is unusually useful: on entering a research collaboration with Lycia, "Carolyn R. Bertozzi, academic founder of Lycia, resigned from the Company's Board of Directors." The company affiliation cost her a Big Pharma board seat; that is the one documented governance consequence found. Lycia was established by Versant Ventures in 2019 inside its San Diego "Inception" labs "in collaboration with academic founder Carolyn Bertozzi," and left stealth in June 2020 with a $50M Versant commitment. The LYTAC work was the asset: Lycia's S-1 (5 October 2026) states "Lycia was founded on the transformational work of Carolyn Bertozzi, Ph.D." Who ran it: Aetna Wun Trombley, PhD, President and CEO from April 2020 — ex-President and COO of NGM Biopharmaceuticals, ex-chief of staff to the CEO of Novartis, ex-McKinsey. Not a Bertozzi trainee; the operating team is industry, heavily NGM and Genentech. The lab lineage shows up on the SAB instead: Steven Banik, first author on the LYTAC paper, now a Stanford assistant professor, sits on it. Her Form 3 for Lycia (22 October 2026) reports isDirector: false, isOfficer: false, isTenPercentOwner: true, and 904,821 shares held directly — the clearest conversion of "academic founder" into a number anywhere in this file, though the ten-percent checkbox cannot be reconciled with a plausible post-IPO share count and should be reported as filed, not as a percentage. Her other companies, with the roles as stated: Thios (2001, co-founder, dissolved 2005), Redwood Bioscience (2008, co-founder with David Rabuka, her former graduate student — the trainee-operator pattern), Enable Biosciences (2015, co-founder, with two of her PhD students per UC Berkeley's IPIRA page), Palleon (2015, now "Co-Founder, Scientific Advisor"), InterVenn (2017, co-founder/advisor; Aldo Carrascoso was the operating founder-CEO), Grace Science Foundation (2018, a non-profit), OliLux (2019). BioCentury counts "at least eight companies since 2000." Olema and Valitor: not found — no evidence links her to either. On the 2022 Nobel: the effect is reputational. Lycia issued a dedicated press release, and the S-1 four years later leads with "Nobel laureate Dr. Carolyn Bertozzi." Her board seats at OmniAb and Alnylam post-date the prize. No evidence that it changed Lycia's valuation, governance, or her role there.

George Church (Harvard). The best single source on what a Church co-founding actually is remains the 2019 Nature Biotechnology feature "One year, 1 lab, 16 spinouts," whose Table 1 — sourced to Church himself — pairs each 2018 company with the Church alumnus who runs it: Dyno Therapeutics/Eric Kelsic, GC Therapeutics/Parastoo Khoshakhlagh and Alex Ng, Manifold Bio/Gleb Kuznetsov and Pierce Ogden, Rejuvenate Bio/Noah Davidsohn, Nebula Genomics/Kamal Obbad and Dennis Grishin, FitBiomics/Jonathan Scheiman, 64-x/Alexis Rovner, Jura Bio/Julie Norville and Elizabeth Wood, and eight more. The article itself distinguishes the roles: "Some 16 Church-cofounded companies came into being last year. And several more added him to their scientific advisory boards." The pricing question has one usable answer, from a founder: "George is a great way to get the flywheel started and to get a big respected experienced name involved with your company. At the end of the day, the business needs to have solid footing for VCs to get interested." (Kamal Obbad, Nebula Genomics CEO.) And one from a co-founder who became an investor — Jorge Conde, now an a16z general partner, who co-founded Knome with Church in 2007: "By spinning out companies, he's running a social experiment to see if an idea is better served in academia or industry. He's certainly not afraid of failure." Church on failure himself: "We put them up on the wall and glorify our failures as challenges for the future." Harvard OTD's Grant Zimmermann supplied the structural observation quoted in point 3 above. Specific roles verified: Dyno — Church co-founder and SAB chairman, Eric Kelsic (his former postdoc) co-founder and CEO, launched with $9M co-led by Polaris and CRV, later a $100M Series A led by a16z. Rejuvenate Bio — the company's own team page says "George Church, Ph.D. — Co-Founder and SAB." Nabla Bio — three co-founders: Surge Biswas (CEO), Frances Anastassacos (President), George Church. GC Therapeutics — Khoshakhlagh CEO, Alex Ng CSO (Church-lab graduate studies), Cory Smith "scientific co-founder," Church co-founder; $65M Series A, ~$75M total. Tessera Therapeutics: not found on his disclosure page — do not call him a founder of it. BGI: SAB since 2007, not a founding.

Jennifer Doudna (Berkeley / IGI). Wikipedia's framing is accurate and matches the primary documents: she "is on the scientific advisory boards of the companies that she cofounded." The single best primary document in this entire file on the founder-versus-adviser distinction is the Editas Medicine S-1 (4 January 2016): "Dr. Doudna was a founder of our company and entered into a consulting agreement with us at the time of our founding"; she "gave notice of termination of that agreement in May 2014 after less than seven months of service" and "has had no further engagement in our business since that time." The same filing says Zhang, Church, David Liu and J. Keith Joung "are founders who continue to provide important scientific guidance and insights to us through ongoing consulting and advisory arrangements" — i.e. all five are founders, and the ongoing relationship is consulting, not management. Elsewhere: Caribou (2011) — co-founded with Rachel Haurwitz, her own doctoral student, who became CEO and president; IPO July 2021 raising $304M gross; reportedly shutting down in October 2026, unable to secure capital to advance its allogeneic CAR-T programmes (single source, worth confirming). Intellia (2014) — she is "one of Intellia's scientific founders"; founding CEO Nessan Bermingham came from Atlas Venture, an investor, and founding CSO John Leonard was AbbVie's former CSO. Scribe Therapeutics — the cleanest "trainees run it, professor advises" case: the company's about page gives "Jennifer A. Doudna, Ph.D. — Co-founder and Scientific Advisor", "Benjamin L. Oakes, Ph.D. — Co-founder, President and CEO" (his Berkeley PhD was done in the Doudna and Savage labs), and "Brett Staahl, Ph.D. — Co-founder and VP of External Innovation" (a Doudna postdoc). Azalea Therapeutics (launched 4 November 2025 with $82M from Third Rock, RA Capital, Yosemite and Sozo) is the newest instance and the role language is precise: Jenny Hamilton co-founder, President and CEO; Connor Tsuchida and Abdullah Syed "scientific co-founders" and VPs; Doudna "Co-founder" and Board Observer, explicitly not a board director.

Feng Zhang (MIT / Broad). Six companies with sourced role language, and the pattern is near-uniform: he is a named scientific founder with no operating role, and the CEO is an industry hire or a trainee. Editas — "one of our founders," and "an employee of Broad in addition to being one of our founders"; CEO Katrine Bosley. Arbor Biotechnologies (2016) — listed under "Founders" on the team page with only his Broad title and no Arbor-specific role; two of the four listed founders, David Scott and Winston Yan, are his trainees; CEO Devyn Smith since 2021. Sherlock Biosciences (2018/2019) — founders listed as Zhang, Jim Collins, Omar Abudayyeh and Jonathan Gootenberg, the latter two Zhang-lab trainees; acquired by OraSure in December 2024. Beam Therapeutics — founders Liu, Joung, Zhang, Nicole Gaudelli and Alexis Komor, the last two David Liu postdocs who developed the base editors; ~$1bn venture pre-IPO, $180M IPO February 2020. Aera Therapeutics — "Feng Zhang, Ph.D. — Scientific Founder and Board Observer," the most precise formulation in the set; CEO Akin Akinc, two decades at Alnylam, not a Zhang trainee. Moonwalk Biosciences — "scientific co-founder," not in the leadership team; the operating team is ex-Illumina (Alex Aravanis, former Illumina CTO, as CEO and co-founder); $57M led by Alpha Wave. And Tome Biosciences, where he was not a founder — see the traps above.

Jim Collins (MIT). The most useful lineage for this group, because the companies are explicitly ML-for-biology and the founder is repeatedly the first author rather than the professor. Integrated Biosciences (2022) answers the brief's question directly: Felix Wong, PhD — "Chief Executive Officer & Co-founder," an NIH-funded independent fellow at the Broad, a Collins-lab postdoc, degrees in applied physics, computer science and mathematics from Harvard, and lead author of "Discovery of a structural class of antibiotics with explainable deep learning," Nature, December 2023; Max Wilson, PhD — CTO and co-founder, an associate professor at UC Santa Barbara; James Collins — "Scientific Co-founder & SAB Chair." The rest of the SAB: David MacMillan (2021 Nobel), Wendy Young (ex-SVP Small Molecule Discovery, Genentech), Uri Alon, and Yuhuai "Tony" Wu (xAI co-founder). $17M announced October 2024; earlier investors Root Ventures, Mission BioCapital, Conscience VC, Reinforced Ventures, Polymath Capital. The credential being capitalised is the first-authorship, not the professorship — and the company has since shifted focus from antibiotics to age-related diseases. Phare Bio (2020) is the same pattern in a social-venture wrapper: Akhila Kosaraju MD as CEO and co-founder, Collins as "Co-founder & Chair of the Scientific Advisory Board," and Jonathan Stokes — first author of the halicin paper and a Collins postdoc — as co-founder, now an assistant professor at McMaster; funded by TED's Audacious Project, ARPA-H (up to $27M) and Google.org. Senti Biosciences (2016) — Timothy Lu, MD PhD, co-founder and CEO; Collins appears on the SAB page in a separate section titled "Scientific Co-Founder." Cellarity (2019) is the exception and the most committed formulation found for any academic: "Jim Collins — Academic Co-Founder and Director, Cellarity" — a board seat. It is a Flagship company, launched by Flagship, with Avak Kahvejian as founding CEO.

Aviv Regev. Exactly one company founding on this evidence, and it is a contrast case. Archetype 2 — The industry-operator founder

Is VP enough? Yes — demonstrably, when paired with a venture firm as co-founder. The cleanest primary proof is Septerna's S-1 (2 October 2024) on Jeffrey Finer, who became President in December 2019 and CEO in November 2021: his top industry titles were "Vice President, Discovery at Five Prime Therapeutics" and "Vice President, Molecular & Cellular Biology and later Vice President, Research Technology at Theravance Biopharma." He had also been a Third Rock venture partner since 2016. VP-level R&D plus venture-partner status at a company-creation firm was sufficient to be founder-CEO of a company that raised a $100M Series A (January 2022, Third Rock-led), a $150M Series B, and then IPO'd. One rung lower: HotSpot Therapeutics was founded by Jonathan Montagu, "the first CBO of Nimbus," and Gerry Harriman, who led Nimbus's ACC programme; neither had been CSO or CMO of a public company; Atlas seeded it in December 2016 and a $45M Series A followed in 2018.

Is "head of a therapeutic area" the bar? Roughly, for the large launches, not for the median one. Jeff Engelman was Global Head of Oncology at the Novartis Institutes for BioMedical Research before co-founding Treeline (≥$473M). Martin Mackay was President of R&D at AstraZeneca and EVP, global head of R&D at Alexion before co-founding Rallybio and serving as CEO. Scott Wasserman was VP, Global Development at Amgen before co-founding Latigo. At the very top: Hal Barron, then President of R&D and CSO at GSK, became CEO of Altos Labs, launched January 2022 with ~$3bn; Marc Tessier-Lavigne, former CSO of Genentech, became founding CEO of Xaira with >$1bn; Stéphane Bancel, CEO of bioMérieux — a diagnostics company, not a pharma R&D organisation — became Moderna's founding CEO.

Is former CSO/CMO of a public biotech the modal profile? No. The distribution is at least trimodal, and the evidence does not support a single mode. The median venture-created launch does not hire a CSO; it hires a mid-career entrepreneur-in-residence. Bruce Booth is explicit that Atlas's founder-EIRs are "typically mid-career drug R&D veterans" and that "most are mid-career by definition"; Atlas's own EIR practice statement is that "the vast majority (>90%) of the Atlas EIRs over the past few years join with significant experience from pharma" or large-cap biotechs. After obtaining his Ph.D., he withdrew from medical school to found Recursion." Age 38 as of December 2020, so about 31 at founding, with no industry experience whatsoever. What investors required alongside the pedigree. The explicit underwriting language is about the team and the models, and never about biology credentials:

  • Nat Friedman called Rives's ex-Meta group a "dream team," and on Rives: "Alex thinks very big. He wants to build a full multimodal model that captures all the complexity of biology."
  • Radical Ventures' Latent Labs page sells the pedigree directly — "realizing purely computational drug discovery," noting that a founder was a key architect of AlphaFold2. Aaron Rosenberg (Radical), who is himself ex-DeepMind Head of Strategy and Operations and helped spin out Isomorphic: "this team will realize the therapeutic and commercial potential of de novo protein design."
  • Edward Kliphuis (Sofinnova): "Latent Labs transforms biology from an observational science into an engineering craft."
  • Alex Lim (IVP, on Cradle): "protein design as a digital service."
  • Elena Viboch (General Catalyst, on Chai): "There are no fundamental barriers to deployment of these models in drug discovery."
  • a16z on Periodic Labs frames the thesis as data generation, not credentials: "The bottleneck has been the iteration speed of human-led experimentation"; "If Moore's Law is slowing, this is how we restart it."

But the structural requirement is visible, and it arrives at or just after the priced round. This is the most consistent pattern in the diaspora data:

  • Chai Discovery: at the $70M Series A, the company's own announcement states that "Mikael Dolsten, M.D., Ph.D., former Pfizer Chief Scientific Officer, is joining the board," foregrounding his record of "150 molecules into clinical trials" and "36 approved medicines." Chai still has no CSO and no scientific advisory board.
  • Isomorphic Labs: a CSO (Miles Congreve), a CMO (Ben Wolf, MD PhD, June 2025), and the most credential-heavy SAB in the set — Jennifer Doudna, David MacMillan, Paul Nurse, Menelas Pangalos (AstraZeneca's former R&D head), Venki Ramakrishnan — three Nobel laureates. Note what this is: Doudna, who appears in this file as an academic founder in her own lineage, is here functioning as borrowed credibility for someone else's company.
  • Bioptimus: an SAB chaired by Sarah Teichmann with three MDs, and a Head of Computational Biology hired out of GSK oncology genomics.
  • Wet-lab validation, in-house or at the customer, is the real diligence gate. Kohl had built DeepMind's wet lab before founding Latent Labs, and Latent Labs runs its own; Cradle's $73M Series B went explicitly into expanding its own wet lab to generate training data; and Chai's pharma contracts were gated on the buyer's own wet-lab work — the GSK release is titled "Collaboration with GSK Following Successful Wet-Lab Evaluation," and Lilly reportedly validated a purpose-built model on its own proprietary data before signing.
  • Counter-examples with no credential layer at all: Latent Labs (no CSO, no SAB) and EvolutionaryScale (none, ever).

Are the seeds systematically larger? Yes — roughly 3x to 25x, and the comparison that matters is within the non-biologist group. Credentialed-biology seeds in the same window cluster at $5M–$14M: Diffuse Bio (a protein-design PhD, YC W23) at under $1M and possibly $130K; 10x Science (three Stanford Bertozzi-lab scientists) at $4.8M; Nabla Bio (two biology PhDs, one from the Church lab) at $11M priced; Tahoe/Vevo (UCSF faculty founders) at $12M; Noetik (an MD/PhD, ex-SVP Research at Recursion) at $14M. AI-lab-diaspora seeds run $30M–$300M. But Cradle is the control that disciplines the interpretation: non-biologist founders from Google product raised €5.5M, while non-biologist founders from DeepMind AlphaFold and Meta ESM raised $40M and $142M. The premium is on a specific brand, not on being a technologist.

Therapeutics or tools? The diaspora clusters almost perfectly on tools, and for at least one company that was the better business. Latent Labs is explicit that it is "not asset-centric" and "won't develop its own therapeutic candidates." Profluent states outright that it "does not plan to develop treatments itself" — and has a Lilly partnership reported by Bloomberg at up to $2.25bn and a BioMarin deal. Chai calls itself "a computer-aided design suite for molecules" and had eight named pharma partners within about eleven months (Lilly, Pfizer, Lilly TuneLab, Novartis, argenx, BMS, GSK, Roche) with no antibody in clinical development. Boltz, nine months after a $28M seed, lists strategic collaborations with Pfizer, Takeda, GSK, dsm-firmenich and Bristol Myers Squibb. Tamarind states that tens of thousands of researchers use it.

And the tools position is fragile. The two bookends are worth stating together. EvolutionaryScale had the strongest pedigree in the sector, no biology credential, no CSO, no SAB, no pipeline and no disclosed pharma revenue — and in November 2025, seventeen months after its $142M seed, the team joined Chan Zuckerberg Biohub; evolutionaryscale.ai now reads "EvolutionaryScale is now part of Biohub," Rives is Head of Science at CZI/Biohub, and terms were not disclosed. Chai added a former Pfizer CSO to its board at the Series A and signed seven pharma partners. The difference between those two outcomes is the clearest available answer to "what did investors require alongside the AI pedigree."

The reverse flow is now visible too: Genesis Molecular AI hired Meta's Sergey Edunov as SVP Foundation Models and then CTO; Cellular Intelligence added Yann LeCun to its SAB. Biology-founded companies are buying the AI pedigree in exactly the mirror image.

Archetype 4 — The repeat founder

The aggregate premium: not found. No study, VC dataset, Nature Biotechnology analysis, Atlas dataset, or PitchBook/SVB founder-level analysis quantifying a repeat-founder premium in biotech was located. LifeSciVC's own site index was searched directly for "founders," "serial entrepreneur" and "founding team" and no Booth post analyses repeat versus first-time outcomes. Say this plainly rather than inferring a premium. What exists is a quantified ladder in individual cases, and two deflationary aggregate findings.

Ken Song — a ~4.6x step-up in first-round equity, immediately after a $4.1bn exit. RayzeBio's S-1 gives his record (Ariosa Diagnostics, which he co-founded and ran 2010–2016, acquired by Roche; Metacrine CEO 2016–2020; Venrock 2007–2010; McKinsey 2000–2002; BS biology MIT, MD UCSF) and the company's own financing history: Series A, August 2020, 45,000,000 shares to nine accredited investors at $1.00 = $45.0M. RayzeBio was itself co-founded by a VC, Aaron Kantoff — whose degrees are a BS in finance and international business from NYU Stern, no science degree — and Song was hired as CEO five months after incorporation. Exit: $4.1bn to Bristol Myers Squibb. Then Candid Therapeutics, whose Form D (filed 13 September 2024) reports totalAmountSold = $206,203,454, with a first sale date of 19 August 2024. The widely reported "$370M+" is, by Song's own description, a combination of several financings, licensing deals and an M&A transaction. The Form D also shows the mechanism concretely: he brought his CFO (Arvind Kush) and his lead investor's partner (Aaron Royston of venBio, who had held venBio's 12.2% of RayzeBio) with him.

Josh Bilenker — a 26x+ step-up. Loxo's S-1: Series A was $10M from Aisling in July 2013 plus $8M from OrbiMed and an Access Industries affiliate in September 2013 = $18.0M total; founder common stock was 120,773 shares to Bilenker for $250; he held 4.5% pre-IPO. Loxo went to Eli Lilly for a reported ~$8bn. Company number two, Treeline Biosciences (founded 2021), was reported by STAT at "at least $473 million" from private investors and headlined by Endpoints as a "$735M 'outer edge' startup," with later filings taking the reported total to nearly $900M and one summary to ~$1.2bn. The sources disagree on which figure is a round versus a cumulative total — do not assert a single number. What is firm: $18.0M first institutional round for company one; a first-money-in of at least $473M for company two.

Ron Renaud — a four-company ladder. Idenix (CFO → CBO → CEO, acquired by Merck); Translate Bio (Chairman and CEO, acquired by Sanofi); Cerevel (President and CEO, acquired by AbbVie for a reported $8.7bn); then Kailera Therapeutics, launched October 2024 with a $400M Series A co-led by Atlas, Bain Capital Life Sciences and RTW, followed by a $600M Series B in October 2025. Kailera is staffed almost entirely from Cerevel and Translate Bio. Same mechanism as Song: the repeat CEO brings an intact team and a known syndicate, and the first round is an order of magnitude larger than a first-timer's.

A distinct archetype worth naming: the serial founder-chairman. Richard Heyman co-founded and ran Aragon (acquired by J&J 2013), then Seragon (acquired by Genentech 2014), then co-founded and chaired Metacrine, then co-founded and was interim CEO of ORIC — a pattern of founding and chairing rather than founding and running.

The two deflationary aggregates: - Bruce Booth, "Grey Hair In the C-Suite" (LifeSciVC, February 2018), on PitchBook data covering 220 biopharma IPOs 2012–2017 (CEO age available for 184): "Average post-IPO returns: founder CEOs 65%, non-founder CEOs 66%." Essentially no founder premium in post-IPO returns, and no correlation between CEO age and returns. Also: median biopharma CEO age at IPO 54; 75% were 48 or older; only 5 of 184 were under 40; software comparison, median 47 with just over 5% in their twenties. - Katrine Bosley, "The Rookie Year and the Biotech CEO" (LifeSciVC, March 2014), n=58 newly public biotechs: 35 of 58 (~60%) were first-time CEOs; 43% of first-time CEOs were founders (15 of 35) against 26% of repeat CEOs (6 of 23); 37 of 58 (64%) of all CEOs were not founders. The implication is counterintuitive and useful: repeat CEOs are markedly less likely to be founders of the company they run. The repeat premium shows up as being hired into companies, not as founding them.

Not verified: Bob Duggan, Vlad Coric, Eric Kelsic's second act, Ted Love, Nick Leschly, Jake Bauer, Deepak Dugar. Also corrected: Ken Song's prior company was RayzeBio, not RAPT.

Is a solo AI founder ever funded in this space? Yes — Latent Labs is the proof, and it is unusual in having no co-founder at all and no SAB. Solo founding is also what produced the single best equity outcome for a founder in this file: Absci's McClain at 11.28% pre-IPO, against the Flagship academic co-founder's under 1%. That is the sharpest contrast in the evidence set.

Who ends up CEO? In the IPO-cohort data, only 3% of CEOs came from academia and the most common prior experience was pharma (39%) or biotech (29%), while the most common prior experience among founders was a senior position in an academic lab. In seed-led venture creation there is often no CEO at all at the start: Booth writes that in most of his seed-led startups "we don't put CEOs in place initially" and "the CSO tends to be the lead member of the management team." He also names the failure mode — "incipient founder-itis," "an expanded, inflated, or inappropriate set of expectations around founder roles" — and his two cautionary archetypes are directly relevant to this group: the scientific founder who directs the science from an academic post while avoiding the key experiments, and the grad-student founder whose drug experience is not biopharma R&D.

Does the CEO type correlate with outcome? On the only quantitative answer available: no. Founder-CEO post-IPO returns 65% against non-founder 66%. Anything stronger than that is not supported by evidence located here, and the group should resist a thesis that rests on it. "The Pairing" reached the same conclusion from a different direction and should be read alongside this file.

Useful context numbers for calibrating a seed. Atlas's initial checks run "$100K to a few $1M," with total seed financing "usually less than $2.5M." The industry median first financing is "the $5–10M range, where it has been for years." Formation has slowed: PitchBook counted "just over 60 new biotechs raising their first round" in Q1 2024, the slowest quarterly pace in eight years, down 50–60% from the 2021 peak. Against that, over $55bn of dry powder across more than 200 life-science VCs. And the cost structure that drives the operator bias: discovery and translational work costs "roughly $25–50M per program and takes 5+ years," and a biotech "may burn cash for 10+ years before any top-line revenue."

Cohort-level outcome data, the only set found. Atlas's biotech seed class of 2013: 19 startups from a $265M fund, the vast majority co-founded by Atlas and incubated in its offices. Four were terminated during the seed process (two for failure to replicate the biology or pharmacology, one for failure to secure academic rights, one for no therapeutic path); three reached a small Series A and then shut down or changed model; of the remaining 13, several were acquired and three went public. Losses averaged less than $2M in Atlas capital; winners averaged closer to 5x that. Deal-weighted loss ratio ~30%; capital-weighted ~10%, against 52% for venture overall. And for the founder-recycling question: "nearly every entrepreneur from a shut-down company moved into another Atlas startup."

Archetype 6 — The outsider

This is the archetype that matters most to this group, so it gets the most careful treatment. The honest finding is not that credential-free founding does not happen. It is that it happens routinely on one side of a boundary and almost never on the other, and that the therapeutics cases which look like exceptions have rented the credential rather than earned it.

Where credential-free is simply normal: tools, software and data for biology

  • Tamarind Bio (YC W24). Deniz Kavi, co-founder and CEO — YC bio: "Building great software for life scientists"; no degrees or employers listed; described elsewhere as a Stanford CS graduate who did computational-biology research at Stanford School of Medicine. Sherry Liu — Stanford computer science, previously AWS. A launch post says the two met as Stanford undergraduates studying CS. Zero biology credentials, no named scientific advisers on the Series A announcement, and a real business: a $13.6M total raise including a $12M Series A led by Dimension Capital with Y Combinator, and YC states that tens of thousands of researchers at pharma companies, biotechs and universities use it.
  • Boltz (PBC). Three MIT CS PhD students, one of whom left his PhD to be CEO, no stated biology training, no named SAB — and five named pharma strategic collaborations (Pfizer, Takeda, GSK, dsm-firmenich, Bristol Myers Squibb) within about ten months of a $28M seed, with Boltz-2 and BoltzGen open-sourced. The substitute credential here is unusually clean and unusually transferable: an open-weights model that the field adopted, shipped before the company existed.
  • Origin (YC W26). Yash Rathod (CS at UIUC, computer vision and RL, first prize in the 2022 OpenCV AI Research Competition) and Malhar Bhide (CS at UIUC; ML research at Wadhwani AI; Automorphic, YC S23; disease-modelling research published in Nature Scientific Reports done in high school). Two CS undergraduates selling licensed patient-derived cancer datasets to drug developers.
  • Halmos Labs (YC F26). Mattias Akke — no degrees listed; ML research internships at MIT's Gómez-Bombarelli lab and at AstraZeneca; a NeurIPS paper on Bayesian optimisation with LLMs. Linus von Ekensteen Löfgren — MS engineering physics, Lund; desk quant at Danske Bank.
  • Menten AI (YC W20). Tamas Gorbe's entire YC bio is "TechBio startup founder with hands-on operational and finance experience"; no degrees or employers listed. What substituted is the cleanest instance of the commercial-validation substitute found: "two paid pilot projects with chemical companies and four letters of intent with pharma and biotech firms."

Where it almost never happens unaided: owning a molecule

The therapeutics cases that look like exceptions, and what each actually rented:

  • Charm Therapeutics — a Nobel-laureate co-founder, full stop. $50M Series A announced June 2022, co-led by F-Prime and OrbiMed with General Catalyst, Khosla, Braavos and Axial (press-reported consistently across many outlets; the company's own site shows investor logos without amounts). The about page has a two-person "Co-founders" section: Laksh Aithani and David Baker. Aithani's stated credential, from the company's own 2022 release: he founded genei, a Y Combinator-backed NLP startup, was "a core contributor to the machine learning platform at Exscientia," and was most recently Entrepreneur in Residence at Braavos. No PhD is attributed to him anywhere found, and no biology credential is claimed in any company material. (Important: no age and no "dropout" claim could be sourced — do not repeat either.) Endpoints' framing of the launch says it all: "Big names, big backers: David Baker joins OrbiMed, F-Prime, Khosla and others as protein-ligand biotech launches." The headline is about the name. And it worked: an $80M oversubscribed Series B in September 2025 with Nvidia participating, a Bristol Myers Squibb collaboration, CHM-029 nominated as a development candidate for AML, and a Clinical Advisory Board announced in August 2026. Note the drift: Charm is now a small-molecule menin-inhibitor oncology company with Gary Glick as interim CEO and Aithani no longer CEO.
  • Cellular Intelligence (formerly Somite AI) — a $575M software exit converted into four National-Academy-class co-founders. Micha Breakstone, CEO and co-founder, described on the company's own site as a "repeat AI entrepreneur who previously built and sold Chorus.ai for $575M," with no biology credential claimed anywhere. The four scientific co-founders, named as such from the outset: Allon Klein (Harvard Medical School, NAS James Prize), Olivier Pourquié (HMS/Brigham, member of the National Academy of Sciences and the National Academy of Medicine), Cliff Tabin (chair of HMS Genetics, NAS), Jay Shendure (UW, HHMI investigator, NAS); Arjun Raj of Penn added as CSO and scientific co-founder in August 2026. Then an MD CMO, an MD Chief Oncology Officer, and an SAB announced 21 September 2026 of Yann LeCun, Bob Langer, Jens Nielsen and Fabian Theis. Funding: >$47M Series A, May 2025, led by Khosla, plus a strategic investment from AMD Ventures. And it bought its way to a clinical asset — a May 2026 agreement with Novo Nordisk to advance STEM-PD, a clinical-stage allogeneic Parkinson's cell therapy. If one example has to carry the whole "credentials are rentable" argument, this is it.
  • GloGlo (YC W24) — a prior exit, a tenured professor, a named adviser, and lived experience. Michael Fester: "PhD research in number theory at Cambridge" — mathematics, not biology — co-founder of Snips, acquired by Sonos, co-founder of 14.ai, and has lived with type 1 diabetes for 17 years. Marie Schneegans: founded 14.ai; Forbes 30 Under 30; no scientific credential of any kind stated. The substitutes are on the same page: Anil Bhushan, PhD — co-founder — Professor of Medicine at UCSF, credited with linking beta-cell senescence to type 1 diabetes; George Eleftheriadis, PhD — Founding Scientist, six years on CAR-Tregs, former senior scientist at Quell where he led the AstraZeneca T1D partnership; Raphaël Scharfmann, PhD — Scientific Advisor, INSERM Research Director, creator of the EndoC human beta-cell lines. The founders brought money-making credibility and the disease; they hired the biology. One unconfirmed but strengthening detail: GloGlo and 14.ai share the YC W24 batch, suggesting GloGlo is a pivot of a non-bio YC slot — inference, not fact. Funding beyond YC: not found.
  • Reverie Labs (YC W18) — the one near-clean case, and the most actionable pattern in the file. Ankit Gupta, founder and CTO — "Previously AB/SM at Harvard in CS." No bio degree, no pharma career, no lab affiliation. Co-founders Connor Duffy and Jonah Kallenbach (credentials not found). The company's YC description is "engineering next-generation, brain-penetrant cancer therapies," it grew to 29 people, and it crossed the boundary from the tools side. Gupta's own account, on his YC profile: Reverie "started by building AI software for pharma companies and ended as a pharma company with its own pipeline of assets built on its ML tools." Sold to Ginkgo Bioworks in February 2024. A structurally interesting coda: Gupta is now a General Partner at Y Combinator and the named YC partner on Convexia, Atlas Discovery and Origin — three of the most credential-light AI-drug companies in YC's portfolio. The gatekeeper for this profile is himself an instance of it.
  • Convexia (YC S25) and Atlas Discovery (YC S26) — read the business model, not the tagline. Convexia bills itself as "the world's first AI-maximalist pharma company"; its founders Ayaan Parikh and Rahul Vijayan studied Stanford CS (and in Parikh's case bio), have no PhD, no pharma career and no lab affiliation, and Parikh has published four papers on molecular dynamics. But its own site sells software subscriptions at $199, $1,497 and $2,997 a month and describes "autonomous AI agents to scout, vet, and accelerate the world's most overlooked drug assets" — asset in-licensing and trial prediction, currently monetised as SaaS, with advisers referenced but none named. Atlas Discovery's founder Shaamil Karim has an entire YC bio of "Excited about AI for predictive biology" — no degree, no employer, no credential listed — and the activity is drug repurposing and clinical trial prediction. Use both as evidence of where the boundary is, not of its breach.
  • WonderTx (YC S26) — the substitute is his own track record. Abraham Heifets' second act, and the YC page sells Atomwise: "raised $220M+, hired 100 people, signed $5B in total partnership value (including Sanofi, Bayer, and Eli Lilly)." Second-time founders in this space do not need to borrow anyone.
  • 1910 Genetics (YC W19) — a genuinely under-documented possible counterexample. Jen Asher, founder and CEO; YC lists no degrees, no prior employers and no credentials; the company is described as an "AI-native biotech focused on discovering small and large molecule therapeutics," 20 people, Boston, founded 2018. Not profiled. Flagged as incomplete, not as a finding.

  • Atomwise. The brief's premise that Heifets and Wallach were CS PhDs is contested by the best available source.   • Diffuse Bio. Namrata Anand is credited by YC with "the first crystal structures of AI-designed proteins." Nothing needed substituting — this is the high-profile-publication case in pure form. It is also, at under $1M raised, the sharpest comparator to the AI-diaspora seeds.

Accelerators as credential laundering: mostly no

  • Y Combinator is the only one with real data, and the data is modest. YC's own directory, pulled via the yc-oss API mirror on 11 November 2026: 40 companies tagged "AI-powered Drug Discovery" out of 6,391 companies all-time — about 0.6%. Drug discovery 35, Therapeutics 44, Biotech 152. The cohort is accelerating (roughly ten companies in the 2025–2026 batches alone) and mortality is visible (Reverie Labs, ResistanceBio, Lavo, Teevox inactive; Known Medicine, PipeBio and Engage Bio acquired, the last by Eli Lilly in 2026). YC's own Request for Startups does not mention biotech, drug discovery or therapeutics at all. The only biology reference is Diana Hu's "Vertical Foundation Models" section — and note the credential implied: "If you know a field well enough to build its model, we'd like to hear from you." Knowing the field, not holding a degree.
  • Entrepreneur First is the most interesting negative result in the file. EF is the purest talent-first, pre-idea, pre-team investor in existence, and its portfolio page lists only three biotech companies in the entire portfolio: Spore.Bio, Ochre Bio and Genomines. If credential-free therapeutics founding were viable at scale, EF is where it would show up. It does not.
  • Nucleate is credential channelling, not laundering. Its Activator forms biotech companies without taking equity — and its five stated application requirements are themselves gates: PI approval, a well-defined technology backed by preliminary research, existing or planned IP, clear application potential, and a team willing to spin out. "PI approval" means you must already be inside an academic lab to apply. No outcome statistics found.
  • Petri (Pillar VC) gives pre-seed capital, coaching and investor access, and publishes no check size, company count or follow-on data; its portfolio page rendered empty. The one AI-drug company tied to it, Nabla Bio, is a credentialed one — two Harvard PhDs. That cuts against the laundering thesis for Petri specifically.
  • IndieBio (SOSV) reports "310 graduates raised $3.6 billion" from 2015 to 2025 — one vendor-reported number, with no check size, cohort size or per-company follow-on rate on the site. Note its own self-description offered pre-seed investment "to scientists ready to launch startups": credential-presupposing.
  • Creative Destruction Lab: not researched. Aggregate data on what fraction of AI-bio seed rounds involve an accelerator: not found — do not let a number into the synthesis.

What is the minimum credential that has actually raised a seed?

Plainly, from the cases above:

For a company that sells tools, models, software or data to drug developers, the minimum credential that has demonstrably raised a seed is: two technical co-founders with undergraduate computer-science training, no biology degree, no PhD, no lab affiliation and no named scientific advisory board. Tamarind Bio is the proof ($12M Series A, Dimension Capital), Origin is a second instance, and Halmos is a third. The things that appear to substitute, in descending order of how often they show up and how much they appear to be worth:

  1. A frontier-AI-lab line on the CV — specifically AlphaFold, Meta ESM, or OpenAI. Worth $30M–$300M at seed, against €5.5M for a non-frontier technologist (Cradle) and under $1M for a protein-design PhD with no AI brand (Diffuse Bio).
  2. A model the field already uses. Boltz raised $28M and signed five pharma collaborations within ten months, on three MIT PhD students and the open-weights Boltz-1/Boltz-2 lineage. This is the most transferable substitute for a group with AI strength and no biology standing: ship something the field adopts before raising.
  3. A prior non-bio exit. Snips→Sonos (GloGlo), Chorus.ai at $575M (Cellular Intelligence), BuildASign (Recursion's Borgeson), and at the extreme the Warp Sciences founding team. A prior exit is the currency non-bio founders most reliably pay the credential with.
  4. Paid pilots and letters of intent from pharma. Menten AI's "two paid pilot projects and four letters of intent" is the clearest instance of commercial validation standing in for scientific standing.
  5. Y Combinator, and it is not nothing — Heifets' testimony is that a credentialed comp-bio PhD could not raise for a year before YC and could after. But note the shape of YC's bio track: 0.6% of all-time portfolio, no mention of biotech in the RFS, and the partner who gatekeeps the credential-light companies is himself a CS-undergraduate founder of one.

For a company that owns a molecule and intends to run a pipeline, the minimum is a credential somewhere in the founding team — owned or borrowed — and no verified case was found of a team with no biology credential and no credentialed co-founder raising a seed to develop drugs. The one case that comes closest, Reverie Labs, did not start as a drug company: it sold AI software to pharma first and earned the right to own assets second, and was then acquired. Everything else that looks like an exception has a professor, a National Academy member, an MD, or a Nobel laureate standing beside the non-credentialed founder on the company's own about page.

The honest framing for the group is therefore not "credential-free founding doesn't happen in therapeutics." It is: in therapeutics the credential is still required, but it can be rented — and non-bio founders pay for it with a prior exit, a model the field uses, or a disease they personally have. The boundary is between selling to drug developers and being a drug developer.

Two further observations the group should weigh against any temptation to rent rather than earn. First, renting is visible to everyone, including the next round: the most reliable pattern in the AI-diaspora data is that the credentialed person arrives at or just after the priced round, which means the seed investors knowingly bought a team that did not yet have it. Second, the two outcomes at the extremes of credential-free therapeutics ambition are instructive in opposite directions — Chai built a $3.8bn tools business by not owning drugs and adding a former Pfizer CSO to its board, while EvolutionaryScale, with the strongest pedigree in the sector and no credential layer at all, was absorbed into a philanthropic research institute seventeen months after a $142M seed.

Supporting questions

Is a PhD effectively mandatory for a scientific founder?

No. A credential is; the PhD is the cheapest way to get one. The empirical literature asked for was largely not found — no Nature Biotechnology analysis of biotech founder credentials, no explicit VC statement that a PhD is or is not required, and a Europe PMC search for academic work on founder credentials and SAB-outcome relationships returned one irrelevant paper. a16z Bio+Health's own landing page is deliberately non-committal, describing its own team as "MDs, PhDs, scientists, and technologists" and treating degrees as one ingredient in a mixed team, never as a founder requirement; the nearest thing to a thesis statement is "A new generation of founders is building a new generation of bio companies."

What exists is the IPO-cohort base rate — 63% of founders in the H1-2021 cohort held a PhD, often with an MD, MBA or JD in addition — and a list of named counterexamples who raised anyway: Sean McClain (BSc, Absci, 11.28% pre-IPO), Ankit Gupta (Harvard AB/SM in CS, Reverie, acquired by Ginkgo), Zlatko Jončev (b12 Labs, no degree listed; three-time national Chemistry Olympiad champion, Roche, EPFL postdoc), Mattias Akke (Halmos, no degree listed, a NeurIPS paper and an AstraZeneca internship), Michael Fester (GloGlo, number-theory PhD research, Sonos exit), Laksh Aithani (Charm, no PhD found, Nobel-laureate co-founder), Gabriele Corso (Boltz, left his PhD), and the Tamarind, Origin, Convexia, Atlas Discovery and Menten founders, none of whom states a PhD.

Scientific advisory boards as borrowed credibility — and do investors discount them?

Yes, explicitly, and the best source is a venture investor writing about his own practice. Bruce Booth, "Biotech Scientific Advisory Boards: What Works, What Doesn't" (LifeSciVC, 10 September 2012):

  • SABs offer "objective, external perspective and quality authentication" and can give a startup "a meaningful stamp of approval to the external world."
  • The economics: "A typical SAB member may charge $2.5-5K per day and receive 0.1-0.3% equity," and "Hollywood-star advisors can be much, much more expensive."
  • The admission: for raw startups an SAB is "sometimes just 'window-dressing' for a story."
  • The discount, in his own words: a "Hollywood SAB-member-in-name-only" can "hurt rather than help" once investors do deeper diligence. He criticises "Hollywood SABs" of Nobel laureates who "haven't done much in years," saying they "look good on paper" while adding cost without shaping the story.
  • Structure and risk: 3–5 core members, 1–2 year renewable terms, and "much of the value from an SAB comes during the informal, regular contacts"; a "strong negative point of view from an influential SAB member can derail programs," and sponsored-research pet projects make programmes hard to kill.
  • Caveat: the post is from 2012; the equity norms may have moved.

Kevin Pojasek's 2015 LifeSciVC guest post (not Booth's — a common misattribution) supplies the function stated approvingly — SABs provide "instant 'brand recognition' among investors and potential partners" — and the cost: "SABs also cost time, money and equity – three of the most precious resources for start-ups." It warns against an SAB built "just to 'check the box'," notes that some companies skip a formal SAB and use consultants instead, and contains the line most damaging to the SAB-as-credential argument: "Having SAB members who have full-time industry day jobs is unusual in biotech where the retired advisor is more common."

Live 2026 exhibits, with the sequencing worth noting. Cellular Intelligence announced LeCun, Langer, Nielsen and Theis in September 2026 — a company whose CEO's credential is a software exit announcing an SAB with a celebrity AI figure and the most-cited bioengineer alive, which is Booth's "Hollywood SAB" as a live press release. Proxima (formerly VantAI) announced its SAB — Raymond Deshaies, Lyn Jones, Juri Rappsilber — two months after an $80M seed: money first, SAB second. Charm announced the "Foundation of Scientific Advisory Board" as a standalone release and a separate Clinical Advisory Board in August 2026. Insilico's only named scientist on the relevant page is Michael Levitt, 2013 Nobel laureate. Isomorphic's SAB holds three Nobel laureates. Has any AI-designed drug reached the clinic, and whose?

Yes, and the registry picture is much thinner than the discourse. All of the following is from direct queries to the clinicaltrials.gov v2 API on 11 November 2026 and is independently verified registry data, not vendor claims.

  • Insilico Medicine is the leader and has the only AI-discovered drug in Phase 3. Eleven registered trials. Rentosertib (INS018_055), a TNIK inhibitor for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, began a Phase 3 on 9 September 2026 (NCT07687459, 320 patients, recruiting, primary completion 30 October 2029). Eight other molecules are in Phase 1 or 2 (ISM5411 in ulcerative colitis, ISM6331, ISM8207, ISM3412, ISM5939, ISM8969). The Phase 2a result is published: "A generative AI-discovered TNIK inhibitor for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis: a randomized phase 2a trial," Nature Medicine 2025;31(8):2602–2610 (DOI 10.1038/s41591-025-03743-2). Read it honestly: n=71 total, 12 weeks, four arms of 17–18 patients; the abstract reports FVC change only for the top dose and placebo (60 mg QD +98.4 mL, 95% CI 10.9 to 185.9, against placebo −20.3 mL, 95% CI −116.1 to 75.6) and gives no FVC result for either 30 mg arm; most common reasons for discontinuation were liver toxicity and diarrhoea. A positive signal in a top journal with a Phase 3 now running, whose headline rests on one arm of eighteen patients. Third-party validation is real but commercial: USP1 out-licensed to Exelixis, KAT6 and KIF18A to Menarini, PHD1/2 Greater China rights to TaiGen.
  • Generate:Biomedicines is the quiet leader in biologics and gets far less attention. Seven registered trials, including two 786-patient Phase 3s of GB-0895 in severe uncontrolled asthma (NCT07276724 from 3 December 2025 and NCT07359846 from 20 January 2026). GB-0669 completed Phase 1.
  • Isomorphic Labs has zero registered clinical trials. A sponsor query returns an empty studies array. Despite ~$2.7bn raised and reported talks at a $40–50bn valuation. (A partner-sponsored trial under Novartis' or Lilly's name would not appear in this query, but Isomorphic itself has registered nothing.)
  • Xaira Therapeutics has zero registered clinical trials, two and a half years after a >$1bn launch.
  • Recursion has ten registered trials and the attrition is legible: three terminations, including REC-3964 in recurrent C. difficile terminated at n=3, and REC-2282 terminated at n=25. The Exscientia legacy confirms the failures: EXS21546 completed Phase 1 at n=64 and its Phase 1/2 combination study was terminated at n=6. DSP-1181 — the molecule that generated the original 2020 "first AI-designed drug in the clinic" headlines — has no record in the registry under Exscientia; its discontinuation is widely reported but could not be verified from a primary source here.
  • Iambic has one trial, and it is substantial: IAM1363 in HER2 cancers, Phase 1, n=383. Absci has one: ABS-201, Phase 1/2, n=227, in androgenetic alopecia; ABS-101 does not appear under an Absci sponsor query. Relay's lirafugratinib now appears in the registry sponsored by Elevar Therapeutics, not Relay — the asset appears to have been out-licensed or divested, which does not seem to have been reported.
  • No FDA- or EMA-approved AI-discovered drug exists as of late 2026, and with rentosertib's Phase 3 primary completion in October 2029, an approval before then is arithmetically impossible.

The skeptical literature, which the synthesis should carry alongside any clinical claim. The best single citation is new: Bender, Thomas, Scannell et al., "Artificial intelligence in drug discovery — what it is, where we stand and the path forward," Nature Reviews Drug Discovery 2026;25(11):855–876 (online 7 August 2026), sixteen authors, whose abstract says the evidence of AI methods' "clinically relevant impact is, so far, disappointingly limited," diagnosing "an insufficient focus on clinical translation during model development," poorly defined problems, and a "technology push rather than science pull" dynamic. Its central recommendation is that benchmarking should measure whether AI tools improve decision-making, not model validation. The commonly cited optimistic counterweight is Jayatunga, Ayers, Bruens, Jayanth and Meier, "How successful are AI-discovered drugs in clinical trials?", Drug Discovery Today 2024;29(6):104009, reporting Phase I success of 80–90% against historic averages and Phase II of about 40%, comparable to historic averages — and it must be cited with its caveats: all five authors are Boston Consulting Group (not BCG/Wellcome; the Wellcome collaboration was the earlier 2022 piece), the abstract states no number of molecules analysed, and the authors themselves hedge the Phase II conclusion as limited by sample size. Underneath both sits Scannell's predictive-validity argument, whose killer number is from 2016: a change of 0.1 in the correlation between a decision tool's output and clinical outcomes can offset a 10- to 100-fold change in brute-force efficiency (PLoS ONE 11(2):e0147215). If predictive validity dominates, AI that generates molecules faster is optimising the wrong variable — and the November 2026 review says the clinical evidence still is not there.

How do venture-creation firms handle founders?

They do not back a founder. They are the founder, and the filings show what that costs the academic.

Flagship Pioneering. The process, in its own words, runs Exploration → ProtoCo → NewCo → GrowthCo; Afeyan's own account is that Flagship launches "about 15 proto-companies a year" of which "six or seven of those become companies a year or two later," that "a proto-company, just like any prototype product, is meant to be beaten up and killed, and then if it fails to die, it becomes a company," and that "we bless them with the indignity of having a number. That way, we can kill them." Academic founders do exist, and the S-1s name them precisely as a distinct category:

  • Moderna's S-1: "Moderna was founded in 2010 by Flagship Pioneering... Our early platform technology was conceived and launched by Flagship Pioneering's VentureLabs (VL) innovation team, led by Dr. Noubar Afeyan... working together with academic co-founders Dr. Derrick Rossi (Harvard Medical School), Dr. Robert Langer (MIT), and Dr. Kenneth Chien (Harvard Medical School)." And the CEO was recruited and first installed as a Flagship partner: "Upon resigning as CEO of bioMérieux, Mr. Bancel became Executive Chairman of Moderna and a Senior Partner at Flagship Pioneering in July 2011. He was then named Moderna's founding CEO in October 2011." Pre-IPO: Afeyan/Flagship 19.5%, Bancel 10.0%, Langer 3.9% — and Rossi and Chien do not appear in the ownership table at all.
  • Generate:Biomedicines' S-1 (filed 4 February 2026) is the best primary source on Flagship founder economics anywhere: "Flagship Pioneering founded Generate in 2018 as Flagship VL56, Inc., working together with Dr. Gevorg Grigoryan, Ph.D., our founding Chief Technology Officer," who "has also held various appointments at Dartmouth College since 2011 (tenured in 2017)." The CEO, recruited in March 2021, was Merck's executive vice president and chief marketing officer — and simultaneously "a CEO-Partner at Flagship Pioneering." Pre-IPO: Flagship-affiliated entities 56.60%; the recruited ex-Merck CEO 6.40%; the tenured Dartmouth professor who is the named co-founder and sitting CTO, 1,502,500 shares — under 1%. Flagship's founding common equity sits in a Flagship LLC, not with individuals. Molly Gibson is named as a co-founder in the narrative and holds no reportable position in the ownership table.

The comparison to state plainly: under 1% for a tenured professor who is a named co-founder and sitting CTO of a Flagship company, against Absci's solo founder at 11.28%, Recursion's Gibson at 42.4% of voting power, and Loxo's Bilenker at 4.5% after $46M raised. The Flagship discount to the academic is real, large, and now documentable from filings.

Third Rock Ventures. Its own approach page describes Discover → Launch → Build, and the mechanism is explicit: at launch its partners "serve in interim roles to execute the company's R&D strategy," and after the interim period the firm will "establish experienced and motivated management teams." Two primary cases: at Relay, Third Rock partner Alexis Borisy was Executive Chairman and interim CEO at the September 2016 launch ($57M Series A from Third Rock plus a D. E. Shaw Research affiliate), and Sanjiv Patel was recruited in early 2017 from EVP and Chief Strategy Officer of Allergan — with the resulting S-1 equity split of SoftBank 40.70%, Third Rock 20.66%, Patel 2.31%, co-founder Mark Murcko (ex-Vertex CTO) 1.56%, and the academic founders Matthew Jacobson and Dorothee Kern absent from the table entirely. At Septerna, the partner simply became the permanent CEO. Septerna's S-1 does disclose that it granted "an additional 14,500,000 shares to our advisors and co-founders outside of the 2021 Plan," so the academic founders were compensated; the exact split was not obtainable from the initial filing.

Atlas Venture. The model is "seed-led venture creation," documented extensively in Bruce Booth's own writing: partner with scientific and business co-founders, write small seed checks, and let the EIR be the founder. ">90% of the Atlas EIRs over the past few years join with significant experience from pharma" or large-cap biotechs; they are "mid-career drug R&D veterans"; "nearly 70% of Atlas's CEOs were first-timers in the role." The single most legible dataset on the resulting shape is Booth's write-up of Nature Biotechnology's best academic startups of 2014: of eleven companies, five were in Atlas Fund IX, and in every Atlas case the academic is the scientific founder while a pharma-veteran EIR is the operator — Quartet (Clifford Woolf, Kai Johnsson; led by Atlas EIR Kevin Pojasek), Synlogic (Jim Collins, Tim Lu; led by Atlas EIR Ankit Mahadevia), Navitor (David Sabatini; led by George Vlasuk, former Wyeth and GSK executive and former Atlas EIR), Unum (Dario Campana; led by Chuck Wilson, "a serial Atlas entrepreneur and former Novartis executive"), Intellia (led by Atlas EIR Nessan Bermingham), Voyager (led by Steve Paul, formerly of Lilly), Editas (led by Katrine Bosley). Booth explicitly rejects the tech-style seed-only model proposed by Y Combinator's Jared Friedman and a16z's Jorge Conde, on the grounds that seed-only investing cannot fund therapeutics at $25–50M and 5+ years per programme.

ARCH Venture Partners and Versant: firm model statements not found — both sites returned 403/404. What is documented: ARCH "jointly incubated" Xaira with Foresite Labs and is named "Founder and lead investor" of Vilya; Bob Nelsen is a Xaira board member and was reported to have sought out Tessier-Lavigne to lead it; Versant established Lycia inside its Inception labs "in collaboration with academic founder Carolyn Bertozzi" and installed an ex-NGM President and COO as CEO. Dimension Capital's own site says only that it is "a multistage, research oriented, investment firm partnering with companies across the frontiers of science and compute" and does not state whether it creates companies, backs founders or recruits CEOs — notable, because Dimension led Tamarind's Series A and was in all four Chai rounds.
