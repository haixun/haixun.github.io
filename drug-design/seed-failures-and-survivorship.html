Evidence file

Seed failures and survivorship: what the dead and the stalled tell you that the living cannot

An evidence file, not an essay. Compiled 11 November 2026 for the ai4bio exploration, as the deliberate correction to any study of what AI-drug-discovery companies "had in hand" when they raised seed money. A study of survivors measures what successful companies looked like; it does not measure what seed investors required, and it overstates the odds by exactly the share of the cohort it cannot see. This file assembles the missing denominator: named failures, documented stalls, quantified base rates, and the gap between reported and independently verified performance. Method: SEC EDGAR (Form D, S-1/424B4, 8-K) and company press archives for primary evidence; the Europe PMC and ClinicalTrials.gov APIs for citations and trial status; FDA's own novel-approvals list for the approval count; trade press where it is the only source, always labelled. Limits: the session's web-search budget was exhausted partway through, so some lines rest on fetched primary documents rather than exhaustive press sweeps; several items are marked "not found" or "status unclear" rather than resolved. Three claims from the group's prior research were put to primary sources and two of them came back changed — see "Corrections to the group's priors."

The short version

  1. Zero approvals, independently re-verified against the primary list. The FDA's own Novel Drug Approvals for 2026 page lists 48 novel approvals between 12 January and 9 November 2026, and not one originates from an AI-native discovery company or is described by its sponsor as AI-discovered. Combined with the field's own literature — ProMarket, July 2026: AI-driven discovery "has not yet produced a single approved medicine" — the group's prior finding of zero stands as of 11 November 2026.
  2. The headline success-rate claim rests on 24 and 10 readouts, and the paper says so itself. The 80–90% Phase I / ~40% Phase II figures trace to one BCG-authored paper (Jayatunga et al., Drug Discovery Today 29(6):104009, June 2024), whose own abstract concedes the Phase II number comes from "a limited sample size." Independent recountings put it at 21 of 24 in Phase I and 4 of 10 in Phase II. Industry Phase 1 comparators range from 47% to 66.4% depending on the source, and 21/24 is not statistically distinguishable from any of them.
  3. And when the sample grew, the advantage shrank and Phase 2 collapsed. Haque et al., Cancer Treatment Reviews 151:103223, 8 October 2026 — peer-reviewed, academic, 129 AI-enabled assets across 69 companies through 1 March 2026 — finds Phase 1 77.8% on n=72 and Phase 2 19.5% on n=24. Against Citeline's 2014–23 baseline of 47% and 28%. Phase 2 went from "40%, comparable to industry" on n=10 to 19.5%, well below industry, on n=24. The cohorts are defined differently and are not strictly comparable, but the direction of travel is the whole story.
  4. Phase I is where AI helps and Phase II is where it does not — and that is the worst possible shape for a seed-stage bet. The one phase AI demonstrably improves (tolerability, molecule quality) is the one that was never the binding constraint. And the headline Phase 2 number is contaminated in the flattering direction: of the six Phase 2 programmes in the BCG set that stopped, only two stopped on negative data — the other four stopped for business or operational reasons (Alacrita, October 2026).
  5. A company can be a partnership failure and a seed success simultaneously, and the field's commentary routinely conflates them. Lycia Therapeutics' Lilly collaboration — $35.0m upfront, five targets, a $2.0m extension fee paid in August 2025, zero milestones ever achieved, terminated without cause effective 12 February 2026 — and Lycia then IPO'd on 22–26 October 2026 at $18.00 for ~$317.5m gross with two Phase 1 programmes. All verified from Lycia's own 424B4. Lilly paid to extend six months before giving notice to kill it. Anyone filing Lycia under "failures" is wrong; anyone filing the LYTAC partnership under "successes" is also wrong.
  6. The best primary-source stall detectors are a missing Form D and, better, a Form D with no press release. Avilar Therapeutics has filed nothing with the SEC in 45 months ($75,276,164 sold to 5 investors, Form D/A 13 February 2023) and has zero ClinicalTrials.gov registrations, ever. Entact Bio took $9,999,997 in January 2026 and never announced it — visible only because 17 CFR § 230.503 compelled the filing. That second pattern is the single highest-signal detectable event in this file.
  7. Deep Genomics is the cleanest quiet-middle case in the field, and it raised $238m. Ten preclinical candidates by 2021; Wilson disease programme halted; dozens of models discarded; zero registered studies on ClinicalTrials.gov, verified by API query on 11 November 2026; no therapeutic candidate, IND or clinical milestone announced since 27 September 2023, everything since being boards, hires, an award and a podcast. Founder Brendan Frey, on the record: "AI has really let us all down in the last decade when it comes to drug discovery."
  8. The translation gap is not an AI problem, which is why AI does not fix it. Roche discontinued GYM329/emugrobart in SMA and FSHD on 23 March 2026 because reducing myostatin "did not translate into the intended functional outcomes" — a conventionally discovered antibody, at Roche, with Phase II/III data, failing at exactly the step AI does not touch, and explicitly not for safety. On 28 September 2026 Roche killed obesity too ("clinically meaningful weight loss is unlikely"), ended all development, and returned every right to Chugai, who is now shopping it.
  9. Startup death leaves no public record by design, which is why the correction in this file cannot be computed. Elizabeth Pollman, Duke Law Journal 73(2):327–386 (2023): "bankruptcy law does not fit the needs of most distressed startups" and their capital structures "are indeed designed to avoid bankruptcy except in unusual circumstances"; failures resolve through "soft-landing acquisitions, acqui-hires, and assignments for the benefit of creditors." The quiet middle is the modal outcome, not a data-collection failure. Even Carta's own shutdown counter (966 in 2024 against 769 in 2023) comes with its author saying "I bet we're missing a good chunk."
  10. Platform-first is the empirically punished model, and the evidence predates the AI boom. Bay Bridge Bio, June 2022, on 500+ biotechs that went public 2010–2022: seven of the ten biggest underperformers were platform companies, and of 32 acquisitions above $1bn, none was a preclinical platform — 40% had an approved product, 31% had filed or were about to, 25% were in pivotal studies. "Good clinical data is the currency of value in biotech."
  11. Independent benchmarking keeps finding that the deep models do not beat the simple ones. Title of Ahlmann-Eltze, Huber & Anders, Nature Methods 22(8), 2025: "Deep-learning-based gene perturbation effect prediction does not yet outperform simple linear baselines." Kedzierska et al., Genome Biology 26:101, 2025: scGPT and Geneformer "could be outperformed by simpler methods" zero-shot. Guo et al., J Cheminform 17:94, 2025: standard scaffold and Butina splits leave train/test similarity high enough to inflate virtual-screening performance; 8,400 models across 60 NCI-60 datasets.
  12. The field's flagship autonomous-discovery result was formally corrected, and the corrected title no longer says "novel." Nature 624:86–91 (2023), "An autonomous laboratory for the accelerated synthesis of novel materials," now carries an Author Correction (Nature 650:E1, published 19 January 2026) whose title reads "...accelerated synthesis of inorganic materials." The abstract claim went from 41 novel compounds from 58 targets (71%) to 36 compounds from 57 targets (63%). The authors concede the novelty claim "was subject to misinterpretation—their intention was to indicate that the materials were new to the prediction platform, not necessarily new to science." The rebuttal said something stronger and remains unrebutted: Leeman et al., PRX Energy 3:011002 (2024) — "These errors unfortunately lead to the conclusion that no new materials have been discovered in that work."
  13. The field's most-cited quantitative evidence that AI accelerates discovery was withdrawn for data-integrity reasons after circulating for seventeen months. MIT, 16 May 2025, on Toner-Rodgers' "Artificial Intelligence, Scientific Discovery, and Product Innovation": "MIT has no confidence in the provenance, reliability or validity of the data and has no confidence in the veracity of the research contained in the paper." Acemoglu and Autor: "at this point the findings reported in this paper should not be relied on." It was never peer-reviewed at any point.
  14. The post-mortems agree with each other to a degree that is itself evidence. Across an outside analyst, a failed founder, a venture investor, a Stigler Center economist, a sixteen-author Nature Reviews Drug Discovery review and an in-house critic, the diagnosis converges on one sentence: the industry automated the step that was cheap. Michael Santoro, ProMarket: "Drugs fail in two ways—the molecule is wrong, or the biological bet behind it is wrong." Bender et al., Nat Rev Drug Discov, August 2026: impact to date is "disappointingly limited", driven by "technology push rather than science pull." Ankit Gupta, who ran one of the two genuinely AI-first companies that died: "with the funding that most techbio companies have, we had the funds for essentially 1 coin flip."
  15. What this does to a study of seed-funded survivors. If roughly 60% of Series A biotechs never reach Series B (Bay Bridge Bio), seed→Series A runs at 25–33% cross-sector for the 2021–22 vintages (Carta), and biotech seed is so thin that 2025 saw 84 disclosed seed rounds against 121 Series As (DealForma), then a sample of visible survivors is drawn from the top decile or two of its cohort — and for biotech it may not even be the same population. Every trait such a sample shares — a named target, a lead series, a pharma partner, a Nature paper — is a trait of survivors, and §(f) shows companies possessing all four have failed anyway. The honest reading of any survivor study: these are necessary-looking but demonstrably insufficient conditions, measured on the winners, with the losers unobserved.

(a) Named failures, shutdowns, wind-downs and pivots

Status vocabulary used strictly below. Dead = confirmed wind-down, liquidation, bankruptcy or dissolution with a primary or named-press source. Absorbed = acquired or merged out of independent existence. Delisted / taken private = equity extinguished or withdrawn from public markets. Pivoted = still operating but no longer developing drugs. Stalled = operating, no confirmed death, no observable progress for a measured interval — the interval is always given. Status unclear = insufficient evidence either way; this is not a synonym for dead.

a1. The most important thing in this section, stated before the table

Almost none of the big, well-documented deaths in computational biology are AI failures. Kaleido died of microbiome chemistry and an FDA warning letter; Zafgen of two patient deaths; Rubius because engineered red cells did not work; Codiak of exosomes; Gritstone of neoantigen immunotherapy economics; Invitae of testing-lab economics plus an acquisition spree; 23andMe of consumer-genomics economics plus a data breach. Every one of those is orthogonal to computation.

In the whole set assembled below, exactly two failures are genuinely computational: Neoleukin's NL-201, a de novo-designed protein discontinued in Phase 1, and Reverie Labs, an AI-first discovery company that was asset-stripped. That asymmetry matters in both directions. It means (i) the field has far less failure evidence about its own core claim than the volume of biotech obituaries suggests, and (ii) the deaths that are available mostly teach biology and capital-market lessons that apply to any biotech. A seed investor looking for evidence that AI-first discovery fails will not find much, because most AI-first companies are too young to have failed yet — which is a statement about cohort immaturity, not about the method working.

a4. The founder post-mortem, which is the best primary evidence in the file about seed-stage AI drug discovery

Ankit Gupta, co-founder of Reverie Labs, "Unfair Coins," 6 January 2025 (ankitg.me). This is a first-hand operator account of exactly the question the group is studying, and every line below is a direct quote:

  • On the original software thesis dying: pharma "doesn't buy much software," so they "became a 'molecules as a service' company."
  • On partnership economics: the Roche deal "took 18 months" to sign, and "pharmas tend to propose their harder targets."
  • On why they were forced to take pipeline risk: "most biotech investors valued these programs at ~$0."
  • On the core structural problem, and this is the sentence to carry forward: "with the funding that most techbio companies have, we had the funds for essentially 1 coin flip."
  • On model quality, from the inside: "even our best AI models were not good enough that we could blindly trust them to synthesize compounds," and "ADMET models have made only marginal improvements."
  • On unfalsifiability — the reported-versus-real gap seen from the operator's chair: "there is not any convincing way to measure how good our coin mint is" other than making and testing molecules; "there are no good benchmark eval sets for the modeling tasks that are actually bottlenecks."
  • On the founding premise being wrong: "the image we had in our head that the design process was deeply computationally driven was mostly not true."

Gupta's "1 coin flip" arrives independently at Jason Steiner's arithmetic in §(e)8 — that a platform needs ~25 candidates for the thesis to pay off — from the opposite end. One is a founder describing what his balance sheet bought; the other is an outside analyst computing what the thesis requires. They do not cite each other and they agree.

a5. The Baker-lab / protein-design record: one failure in eleven companies

Checked against the Institute for Protein Design's own translation page, which is the authoritative list and names 11 spinouts with two exits, one merger and no closures: Cyrus Biotechnology (2014), PvP Biologics (2016, acquired by Takeda 2020), Icosavax (2017, acquired by AstraZeneca 2024), A-Alpha Bio (2018), Neoleukin (2018, merged into Neurogene 2023), Mopac Biologics (2020), Monod Bio (2021), Lila Biologics (2022), Vilya (2022), Archon Biosciences (2023), Skape Bio ApS (2025).

Zero closures across 11 companies in 11 years. That is itself a finding, and it cuts against the expectation this file was written to test: the protein-design translation record is one of durable companies and modest exits, with exactly one public-market failure (Neoleukin).

Checked individually and no failure found: Cyrus Biotechnology (site current, three named programmes, co-founded the OpenFold consortium; spun out Levitate Bio in June 2024 while keeping therapeutics — a narrowing, not a failure), A-Alpha Bio, Monod Bio (tools/diagnostics, shipping products), Vilya (Vilya-2 all-atom foundation model 28 Jul 2026; Science paper 25 Apr 2024; furthest programme integrin α4β7 for IBD; note the domain moved vilyatx.com → vilya.ai and it dropped "Therapeutics" from its name), Xaira Therapeutics (news through 23 Oct 2026; first pipeline look 2 Oct 2026), Arzeda (a 2008-vintage Baker spinout that went industrial, not pharma — enzymes for food, home care and materials, with named deals with MANE, Unilever and KENT; a de facto pivot away from therapeutics executed long ago and apparently successfully).

Icosavax deserves a correction to the framing in the group's brief: it was a modest win, not a salvage. Series B $100m led by RA Capital; IPO 28/29 July 2021 at $15.00, upsized to $209.3m gross, trading ~150% above IPO on debut. AstraZeneca announced acquisition 12 Dec 2023, completed Feb 2024: $15.00/share cash (~$800–838m upfront) plus a non-tradable CVR of up to $5.00/share — ~$1.1bn total, a 91% premium including the CVR, ~43% on the upfront alone. A 43–91% premium with a strategic buyer taking the asset forward is not a distress sale. But note the exact arithmetic: the upfront price of $15.00 is precisely the IPO price, so IPO buyers broke even nominally over 2.5 years and are made whole only if the CVR pays; anyone who bought above $15 lost. A win for venture, a wash for the listing, and a genuine success for the science — IVX-A12 was a Phase 3-ready, Fast Track, computationally designed VLP combination vaccine. Still alive: EMA agreed a paediatric investigation plan 28 Jan 2025 and a Phase 3 in adults 60+ is registered with AstraZeneca AB as sponsor. No discontinuation found.

(b) The quiet middle: companies that are neither dead nor moving

This is the more common outcome than death and the more informative one, because it is the outcome a seed investor is actually exposed to. A company that dies loudly produces a filing, a press release and a Fierce Biotech obituary. A company that stops working produces nothing at all, keeps its website up, and remains in every "companies to watch" list indefinitely.

b1. How to detect it: nine signals, ranked by how hard they are to fake

The most useful methodological finding in this file is that the quiet middle is detectable from public, dated, falsifiable records — you do not need inside information, and you do not need to rely on the absence of press releases, which is the weakest signal.

The reason these work is that three of them are backed by filing obligations with statutory deadlines, which converts "the website looks stale" into "a required filing is missing." The regulation text was pulled directly in each case.

1. The Form D gap and — more valuable — the Form D with no press release. Strongest signal in the file. 17 CFR § 230.503. The initial notice is due "no later than 15 calendar days after the first sale of securities in the offering" (§ 230.503(a)(1)), and an annual amendment is due "on or before the first anniversary of the filing of the notice of sales on Form D" or of the most recent amendment, "if the offering is continuing at that time" (§ 230.503(a)(3)(iii)). So Form D is a near-mandatory, dated, free, structured record of every priced private round, and it has two uses:

  • No new Form D in N years = no new priced equity, whatever the website implies.
  • The inverse, and the better one: a Form D with no press release = an unannounced insider bridge. This is a disclosure a company is legally compelled to make and commercially motivated not to publicise, which makes it the highest-signal detectable event available. (Worked example: Entact Bio in b2.)

The XML also gives total offering, total sold, date of first sale, number of investors, and every executive officer and director by name — so comparing officer lists across consecutive filings is a free founder-departure detector needing no paywalled source. And it exposes the gap between the announced round and the amount actually sold (Firefly Bio: "$94m" announced, $40.0m sold on a $72m offering).

Honest limits: a Form D is not strictly universal — Section 4(a)(2) offerings without general solicitation need not file — and Vicinitas has none at all despite a $65m Series A. Absence is therefore weak on its own. It is strong only as a change in a company that has filed before. Free endpoints: sec.gov/cgi-bin/browse-edgar?action=getcompany&company=<name>&type=D → data.sec.gov/submissions/CIK##########.json → .../Archives/edgar/data/<cik>/<accession>/xslFormDX01/primary_doc.xml.

2. ClinicalTrials.gov registration staleness or absence. 42 CFR § 11.64. Registration information "must be updated not less than once every 12 months" (§ 11.64(a)(1)); the Record Verification Date must be refreshed at least every 12 months "even if no other updated information is submitted at that time" (§ 11.64(a)(1)(ii)(N)); and recruitment status changes must be posted within 30 calendar days. Therefore an "ongoing/Recruiting" trial whose LastUpdatePostDate is more than 12 months old is a missed legal obligation, not merely stale data. Query it free: clinicaltrials.gov/api/v2/studies?query.spons=<name>&fields=OverallStatus,LastUpdatePostDate,StartDate&countTotal=true. And totalCount: 0 for a company that says it is "advancing toward the clinic" is itself the finding — it came back 0 for Avilar, Entact Bio, Firefly Bio, Vicinitas and Deep Genomics.

2b. Trademark abandonment. 37 CFR § 2.160. A declaration of continued use is due "on or after the fifth anniversary and no later than the sixth anniversary after the date of registration", with a six-month grace period, and if not filed the registration "will be cancelled." A §8 lapse at the five-to-six-year mark is a dated, binary, company-initiated admission that a brand is no longer in use — well matched to the 2019–2021 techbio vintage. This method could not be demonstrated: USPTO's TSDR API now requires a key and tmsearch.uspto.gov is session-gated. Recorded as a method worth wiring up, not as data in hand.

3. The "zombie rate" definition, from a practitioner. Bay Bridge Bio (Richard Murphey), "How are Series A biotech VCs performing?", updated 9 December 2021, operationalises it as: more than two years without raising more money or completing a cash-generating partnering deal with a larger pharma company. This is the most precise definition located anywhere in this search, and the two-year threshold is the one to use. (The per-fund percentages exist only inside chart images on that page; no numbers are in the text, so no range can be quoted.)

4. Composition of press releases, not their frequency. The useful test is not "has it published?" but "has it published about the science?" A feed consisting of board appointments, advisory-board additions, executive hires, awards and podcasts, with no therapeutic candidate, IND or clinical milestone, is a company whose communications function is working and whose pipeline is not. (This is the single most discriminating signal in b2, and it is why the "press blackout" framing in the group's prior research should be generalised to "science-release blackout" — a company with an active board can look busy for years.)

5. Website staleness at page granularity — the underrated one, and the method that produced the best single timeline in this file. Most biotech sites are WordPress or Webflow and leak per-page timestamps. https://<domain>/wp-json/wp/v2/pages?per_page=100&_fields=title,link,date,modified&orderby=modified&order=desc returns a last-modified date for every page. On Avilar this produced the entire sequence in b2 — including that the science pages froze in 2022–2024 while the leadership page was edited the day before the CEO announcement. That is far better evidence than "the site looks the same." Fallback: curl -s https://<domain>/sitemap.xml | grep -oE '<lastmod>[^<]*', which worked on entactbio.com (max 2026-08-13), bighatbio.com (2026-10-27), somite.ai (2026-11-08) and integratedbio.com (2026-11-10), and returns nothing at that path for Webflow/Framer sites — try /sitemap_index.xml and robots.txt.

Press-page archaeology, done properly: count the items and measure the largest inter-item gap, do not just look at the newest date. And read the "In the News" page separately from the press-release page. On Avilar, third-party coverage stopped 43 months ago, fourteen months before its own releases did — i.e. press attention died first. That ordering is probably generalisable and is worth testing on other cases.

6. Domain lifecycle — high signal, but it needs rules, because it produces false positives readily. Registry WHOIS creation dates plus actually fetching the content, both free and instant. The observed patterns, each with a real example:

  • Lapse-and-resquat (strongest). vicinitastx.com shows Creation Date 2026-05-05, registrar Chengdu Fly-Digital, and now serves <html lang="zh-CN"><title>站点正在建设中</title> ("site under construction"). A creation date after the company was founded cannot be a registrar transfer — WHOIS creation dates survive transfers — so the registration died and a third party took it.
  • TLS failure on a resolving domain. reverielabs.com and interlinetx.com both resolve (AWS) but return curl code 000 — no TLS handshake. Site torn down, DNS not cleaned up.
  • Nameservers delegated to an acquirer, no A record. carawaytherapeutics.com sits under Merck's msddns.com; serotiny.bio under ns01.jnjdns.com (Johnson & Johnson). Defensive retention post-acquisition — site death is sometimes the best outcome available.
  • Parked for sale. Afternic or GoDaddy /lander nameservers.

The two false positives that were actually hit, and the rule they produce. charmtherapeutics.com is not registered at all, which reads as catastrophic — but Charm Therapeutics simply renamed its domain to charmtx.com, which is live and has CHM-029 recruiting in Phase 1 (NCT07751991, start 28 Sep 2026, last updated 6 Nov 2026). And shenandoahtherapeutics.com parks at a GoDaddy lander because Shenandoah is a 2025-vintage startup that bought the domain and has not built the site — it has $21.5m from 12 investors with Nathanael Gray and Wendy Young on the board. Rule: never score a domain signal without a companion check on EDGAR and ClinicalTrials.gov, and always try obvious alternate spellings first. A missing website is evidence of a missing website.

7. Copyright years are worthless. Menten AI's site reads "© Menten AI 2025," which would flag it — while its news page shows activity through 16 April 2026. Worse, its press list contains seven items all stamped "September 8, 2025" whose content is from 2020, a CMS migration artefact. Scraped dates lie; spot-check them.

8. Investor portfolio-page removal — usable, with a hard caveat. Interline Therapeutics appears nowhere on Foresite Capital's portfolio page, not even under "(Alumni)" — and that page does retain dead companies (Velia, which shut in 2025, is listed as Alumni), so the total absence is anomalous. Conversely Avilar is still on RA Capital's portfolio page with no exit label, on a page that uses explicit "Acquired by…"/"Now…" labels elsewhere — so the lead investor has not written it off publicly. Caveat: many VC portfolio pages are fully client-rendered. Versant's, a16z's and Droia's all defeated extraction entirely. A negative result is meaningful only when you can confirm the full list rendered.

9. Identity migration. A rename from "X Therapeutics" to "X AI", or a domain move from .com/tx.com to .ai/.ml, is dated and cheap to check — §a9 records three instances. It signals repositioning away from owning drugs, which is different from failure but is often the step before the pipeline stops being mentioned.

Signals that could not be executed, recorded so the gap is visible. LinkedIn headcount decay — requires authentication, unreachable; this is the single most-cited signal in the practitioner literature and there is no first-hand data on it in this file. State entity status / franchise-tax forfeiture — conceptually the best available signal, since a Delaware "void" status or a lapsed annual report is an unambiguous, dated, company-caused admission; California's business-search API sits behind Imperva, Delaware's ICIS is captcha-gated, Massachusetts is form-POST only. Needs a browser or a paid source. WARN notices — mostly moot anyway, since sub-100-employee preclinical startups fall under the thresholds, which is exactly the cohort in question. Job-posting disappearance — checked by hand for Avilar (careers page empty), not systematised.

b1a. Why the quiet middle exists at all: the load-bearing citation

The most important source in this section is not a methodology but a legal analysis, because it explains that startup death normally leaves no public record by design.

Elizabeth Pollman, "Startup Failure," Duke Law Journal 73(2):327–386 (2023); ECGI Law Working Paper No. 729/2023. From the abstract:

"no scholarly account systematically explains what happens to these startups at the end of their life cycle"

"bankruptcy law does not fit the needs of most distressed startups" … "their capital structures are indeed designed to avoid bankruptcy except in unusual circumstances"

failures resolve through "soft-landing acquisitions, acqui-hires, and assignments for the benefit of creditors", letting founders "'fail with honor' and redeploy their talent and capital into other ventures."

Bankruptcy generates a docket, press coverage and a date. An assignment for the benefit of creditors, a quiet dissolution and an acqui-hire generate none of those. So the quiet middle is not a measurement failure that better data would fix — it is the modal outcome, and the detection problem follows directly from the capital structure. (Caveat: only the abstract was readable; the Duke PDF 403s and the ECGI links resolve to metadata pages. Read the body before quoting beyond this.)

A note on the practitioner "zombie startup" checklists, of which there are many: they are inward-facing and SaaS-shaped — burn versus ARR growth, monthly churn thresholds, CAC-to-LTV ratios, "no new logos in six months," weekly growth rules traceable to around 2013. None is observable from outside the company, and none transfers to preclinical biotech, which has no revenue, no logos and no churn. Do not build a method on that literature. The one transferable idea from the adjacent stealth-detection work is to treat the question as a mosaic rather than a single signal, and to watch specifically for a Form D or database entry with no accompanying press release — which is the Entact Bio pattern below.

Why this matters more than the headline number. Series A → B at ~40% is measured after the seed→A gate has already removed companies. The compound rate from seed to Series B is therefore materially below 40%, and the seed→A rate on its own is the figure the group's question most needs. It was not obtained for biotech specifically in this session — see "Gaps and conflicts."

The capital arithmetic behind the 40% (Bay Bridge Bio, April 2023, all sector-level, no n given): an ~$8bn shortfall of follow-on capital for Series A companies versus the prior year; the IPO market down nearly 90% from 2021 to 2022, cutting expected IPO returns from ~1.5× to ~0.1×; IPOs had supplied $10bn+ per year at the peak and that supply largely disappeared; XBI down ~50% from its February 2021 peak; pharma M&A capacity able to buy only ~10% of each startup, which the author notes is "not enough to return investors' capital." Phase III companies retained most of their value; earlier-stage companies did not.

The stall metric, defined by a practitioner. Bay Bridge Bio, "How are Series A biotech VCs performing?", updated 9 December 2021, defines a "zombie rate": the share of a fund's portfolio that has gone more than two years without raising more money or completing a cash-generating partnering deal with a larger pharma company. Cohort: lead-Series-A investors with ≥7 deals since January 2018, over investments and exits from January 2018 to June 2021. The per-fund zombie percentages appear only in charts and are not stated in the page text, so no numeric range can be quoted. The definition is the usable output — it is the most precise operational definition of the quiet middle found anywhere in this search, and §(b) uses it.

c3b. Published criticism of the AI success-rate claim, collected

Alacrita Consulting, "Deterministic Intelligence in a Probabilistic World," October 2026 (Anthony Walker and Cort Hepler) — consultancy, and it is the source that reconstructs the BCG sample sizes. Its four objections:

  1. Samples of 24 and 10, with data only through December 2023.
  2. Phase 2 at 40% is at, not above, historical norms.
  3. The decisive one: of the six Phase 2 programmes that stopped, only two stopped on negative data. The other four stopped for business or operational reasons. So the "40%" conflates scientific attrition with commercial attrition — and in the direction that flatters the technology, since a programme killed for lack of money is counted as a failure of the drug.
  4. Companies advance their best-characterised molecules first regardless of AI, which inflates early-phase rates in both analyses.

(d) The reported-versus-real gap

The structural problem is simple to state and hard to escape: in AI drug discovery, the party that makes a performance claim is almost always the only party with the data, the model, the compounds and the assay. Claims are therefore company-reported, unblinded and unreplicated by construction, and the field has very little machinery for changing that. This section separates four things that are usually run together: published critiques of the standard; cases where a claim did not survive scrutiny; benchmark contamination and leakage; and the one genuinely good counter-example.

d1. Published critiques of the field's validation standards

The load-bearing theoretical result, and it is the most important citation in this file. Scannell JW & Bosley J, "When Quality Beats Quantity: Decision Theory, Drug Discovery, and the Reproducibility Crisis," PLoS ONE 11(2):e0147215, 10 February 2016. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0147215, PMC4749240, open access. It proves, by decision theory rather than by anecdote, that the entire AI-for-discovery value proposition is aimed at the wrong variable:

"We find that when searching for rare positives (e.g., candidates that will successfully complete clinical development), changes in the predictive validity of screening and disease models that many people working in drug discovery would regard as small and/or unknowable (i.e., an 0.1 absolute change in correlation coefficient between model output and clinical outcomes in man) can offset large (e.g., 10 fold, even 100 fold) changes in models' brute-force efficiency."

"For much of the parameter space illustrated, an absolute 0.1 change in ρ_Y,R, the correlation coefficient, has a larger effect on PPV than a ten-fold or 1 log₁₀ unit change in throughput."

And the reason the industry optimises throughput anyway, which is a management finding dressed as a methodological one:

"After all, brute-force efficiency metrics are relatively easy to generate, to report up the chain of command, and to manage."

"PMs that may be competently reported and reproducible in a narrow technical sense can fail because their domains of validity are too narrow; they 'work', but are not usefully generalizable."

The follow-up, 2022, which the group should treat as the canonical statement. Scannell JW, Bosley J, Hickman JA, Dawson GR, Truebel H, Ferreira GS, Richards D, Treherne JM, "Predictive validity in drug discovery: what it is, why it matters and how to improve it," Nature Reviews Drug Discovery 21:915–931, 4 October 2022. DOI 10.1038/s41573-022-00552-x:

"Successful drug discovery is like finding oases of safety and efficacy in chemical and biological deserts. Screens in disease models, and other decision tools used in drug research and development (R&D), point towards oases when they score therapeutic candidates in a way that correlates with clinical utility in humans. Otherwise, they probably lead in the wrong direction. … the detectability of good candidates is extremely sensitive to predictive validity, because the deserts are big and oases small. Both history and decision theory suggest that predictive validity is under-managed in drug R&D, not least because it is so hard to measure before projects succeed or fail later in the process."

Nothing newer from Scannell was located, and no Scannell writing specifically about AI-drug-discovery claims was found — his argument is applied to AI by others, not by him.

The Nature correction concedes identification ambiguity in 4 of 40 and a mis-stated novelty framing. It does not concede Leeman et al.'s conclusion that nothing new was made. The reason this case matters beyond materials science: the robotics worked and the automated interpretation did not, and the fix was a human performing the Rietveld refinement by hand. That is the same failure mode as an unblinded in-house assay readout in drug discovery — the automation is real; the claim about what it found is the part that needed an independent party. And the generalisation is not isolated: Cheetham AK & Seshadri R, "Artificial Intelligence Driving Materials Discovery? Two things follow for a seed-stage reader. First, the split is a choice made by the claimant, and in the one case where a third party changed it systematically (Guo et al.) the measured difficulty of the benchmark moved substantially. Second, the pattern is not "deep learning does not work" — it is "the reported margin over a simple baseline is a function of who chose the evaluation." A seed claim of the form "our model achieves X on benchmark Y" is uninterpretable without knowing who built the split.

"We demonstrate that splitting by protein-sequence identity is inherently insufficient to prevent data leakage due to 'target mirroring,' in which homologous proteins with low overall sequence identity still exhibit highly correlated binding profiles. Our meta-analysis of documents in the ChEMBL 36 database identifies more than 6,000 such assay pairs and finds that leakage persists for sequence-identity thresholds as low as 0.2, well below the values commonly used in benchmarks today."

"a ligand-only baseline model, which lacks protein structural information, achieves surprisingly high performance on the FEP+ 4 and OpenFE benchmarks (r = 0.66 and r = 0.36, respectively)."

"Our results indicate that current benchmarks tend to reward models for memorizing training data and exploiting localized leakage rather than truly learning biophysical principles."

In the hardest tier of their proposed Novelty-Tiered Affinity Benchmark (Tanimoto < 0.35), the ligand-only baseline drops to r = 0.14. A model that cannot see the protein at all scores r = 0.66 on a standard FEP benchmark. That single number is the most compact statement of the reported-versus-real gap in this file.

The canonical chain on virtual-screening benchmark bias, which has been accumulating since 2018 and is routinely ignored:

  • Wallach I & Heifets A, "Most Ligand-Based Classification Benchmarks Reward Memorization Rather than Generalization," J Chem Inf Model 58:916–932, 26 April 2018, DOI 10.1021/acs.jcim.7b00403: "the amount of AVE bias strongly correlates with the performance of ligand-based predictive methods irrespective of the predicted property, chemical fingerprint, similarity measure, or previously applied unbiasing techniques. Therefore, it may be the case that the previously reported performance of most ligand-based methods can be explained by overfitting to benchmarks rather than good prospective accuracy." (Note: both authors were at Atomwise, i.e. this critique came from inside an AI-discovery company.)
  • Sieg J, Flachsenberg F & Rarey M, "In Need of Bias Control," J Chem Inf Model 59:947–961 (2019), DOI 10.1021/acs.jcim.8b00712: "In most cases, it is unknown which features in the data are decisive and whether a model's predictions are right for the right reason. … we demonstrate on two examples from current literature that bias is learned implicitly and unnoticed from standard benchmarks."
  • Chen L et al., "Hidden bias in the DUD-E dataset leads to misleading performance of deep learning in structure-based virtual screening," PLoS ONE 14:e0220113 (2019), DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0220113, PMC6701836: "superior enrichment efficiency in CNN models can be attributed to the analogue and decoy bias hidden in the DUD-E dataset rather than successful generalization of the pattern of protein-ligand interactions," and their enrichment "not superior to the performance of the docking program AutoDock Vina."
  • Tran-Nguyen V-K, Jacquemard C & Rognan D, "LIT-PCBA: An Unbiased Data Set for Machine Learning and Virtual Screening," J Chem Inf Model 60:4263–4273 (2020), DOI 10.1021/acs.jcim.0c00155: "artificially constructed ligand sets classically used by the community (e.g., DUD, DUD-E, MUV) are unfortunately biased by both obvious and hidden chemical biases, therefore overestimating the true accuracy of virtual screening methods."

The reproducibility audit of a public leaderboard, and the result is brutal. Koleiev I et al., "An End-User Audit of Reproducibility, Data Leakage, and Overfitting of the Top-Ranked ADMET Prediction Models in TDC Leaderboards," J Chem Inf Model 66:8045–8058 (2026), DOI 10.1021/acs.jcim.6c00819, PMC13417885. They took all 22 TDC ADMET leaderboards, the top three models in each, and tried to reproduce them as an end user with only the public artefacts:

"Only three models (CaliciBoost, MapLight, and MapLight + GNN) passed all stages and reproduced their reported performance. The remaining models failed because of unavailable code, nonreproducible environments, runtime incompatibilities, or methodological flaws. We traced direct or indirect data leakage in the MiniMol, GradientBoost, and XGBoost models, and used deliberately overfitted variants of our own Mol2Vec-based models to show that tuning on the public test set—whether accidental or intentional—can substantially inflate both metrics and leaderboard rank. These results indicate that current TDC leaderboard positions cannot be read as a direct measure of model quality and practical applicability."

Three of twenty-two reproduced. If a seed pitch cites a leaderboard position, this is the prior to apply to it.

Property prediction and the split problem, with the measured effect size. Pat Walters, "Some Thoughts on Splitting Chemical Datasets," 16 November 2024, shows that scaffold splits themselves leak — two molecules differing by one atom can have different scaffolds yet Tanimoto 0.66, so "predicting the properties of molecule 2 becomes trivial." His measured mean R² differences between split strategies are ~0.05–0.11, ordering random > scaffold > Butina > UMAP (UMAP significantly worse than all others, Tukey HSD) — consistent with Guo et al. above, and from the opposite methodological direction. He adds: "a time-based split of the data is ideal … However, most commonly used benchmark datasets don't have time stamps, making a time-based split impossible." Related: Ferri P & García-Gómez JM, J Chem Inf Model 66:6837–6854 (2026), DOI 10.1021/acs.jcim.5c02891, name "overrepresentation bias" in blood-brain-barrier prediction and measure it: "13.3% in average for the area under curve and 16.44% in average for the macro F1-score."

d4. The counter-example: what good validation looks like when it exists

Worth recording, because the field's critics rarely name a working mechanism. Fischer Y, Southiratn T, Triki D, Cedeno R, "Deep Learning vs Classical Methods in Potency and ADME Prediction: Insights from a Computational Blind Challenge," J Chem Inf Model 65(24):13115–13131, 2025 (DOI 10.1021/acs.jcim.5c01982, PMID 41325513). The 2025 ASAP–Polaris–OpenADMET Antiviral Challenge was prospective and blinded, with 65+ participating teams and a common held-out answer key. The authors' own submissions placed first for pIC50 against SARS-CoV-2 Mpro and fourth for aggregated ADME. Their finding is mixed in a way no company press release would produce: classical methods remain highly competitive for potency prediction, while deep learning significantly outperformed traditional machine learning for ADME.

This is the shape of evidence the rest of the section is complaining about the absence of — prospective, blinded, multi-entrant, with a result that cuts both ways. There is no equivalent for target selection, for de novo binder design against a novel target, or for anything downstream of a molecule. No blinded, prospective, multi-party challenge for AI target identification was found. Its absence is the single largest hole in the field's evidence base, because target selection is where §(e) says the failures actually happen.

(e) Why the failures failed: recurring causes

The group's prior research noted that post-mortems in this field agree with each other unusually often. That holds up, and it is worth saying why it matters: when an outside analyst, a founder of a failed program, a venture investor, an academic economist, two independently written 2026 peer-reviewed reviews and a practitioner-critic converge on the same diagnosis without citing each other, the diagnosis is probably not a narrative artefact. Eight causes recur. They are ordered by how often they appear and how load-bearing they are, not by how often they are named in press releases.

1. The wrong bottleneck — automating the cheap step

This is the single most-repeated finding, and it is structural rather than executional. The costly, uncertain part of drug development is whether the biological hypothesis is right; the part AI accelerated is the generation of molecules against a hypothesis already chosen.

  • Michael A. Santoro (Santa Clara University; ProMarket / Stigler Center, 2 July 2026): "Drugs fail in two ways—the molecule is wrong, or the biological bet behind it is wrong." His framing is that AI has addressed the first failure mode "and barely touched the second," and that molecule design "was never the costly part." He prices the stages: Phase I ~$4m, Phase II ~$13m, Phase III $20m+, against ~$2.6bn to bring a drug to market — the money is downstream of everything AI improved.
  • Khairil et al., Pharmaceuticals 19(6):916 (10 June 2026), peer-reviewed, concluding: "The major disconnect between computational acceleration and biological validation represents the defining limitation."
  • Rachel DeVay Jacobson, npj Drug Discovery 2(1):10 (4 June 2025): "AI efforts so far have not resulted in more effective drugs," with the observation that most AI effort went into molecule optimisation rather than into understanding human biology, and that fewer than 24% of AI companies self-report using AI to study human health and disease during preclinical development.
  • Jason Steiner, Timmerman Report (2 January 2024), gives the structural version: "a platform's value shows in its second success," which is unreachable when the first has not happened.
  • This is also the group's own prior finding from Daphne Koller ("manufacturing keys… for the wrong locks") and from Jackie Hunter at BenevolentAI in 2016 — ten years before the failures it predicted. The diagnosis was available from the start and was not acted on.

2. Train in one system, validate in another

A distinct and more mechanical version of cause 1, and the clearest statement of it comes from an operator rather than a critic. Francisco LePort and Martin Borch Jensen, Gordian Biotechnology (20 November 2024): "Drug discovery has, and still does, take place in one system, but validates those discoveries in a different system." Models are trained on data from cultured cells, organoids, explants and tissue slices because in vivo work is "expensive, slow, and does not scale" — and then the prediction must hold in a living mammal. They count 11 of 12 steps in a generic preclinical process occurring outside the animal model. (Note the conflict of interest: Gordian sells in vivo screening. The argument stands on its own logic and is corroborated by Jacobson's independent complaint about "simple, often non-physiological, readouts from genetically identical cell lines or animals.")

3. Platform without a product — and the empirical penalty is measured

The business-model failure is not a matter of opinion; it was quantified before the AI cohort existed. Bay Bridge Bio (Richard Murphey), "Biotech platforms are out, products are back in," 20 June 2022, on 500+ biotechs that went public via IPO or SPAC from 2010 to 2022:

  • Seven of the ten biggest underperformers were platform biotechs. Five of the ten biggest losers by market cap were SPACs and five were techbio, although SPACs were only ~5% of new public-market entrances and techbio ~5% of biopharma entrances — i.e. both were over-represented among the losers by roughly ten-fold.
  • Of 32 acquisitions above $1bn in that period: 40% had an approved product, 31% had filed or were about to file, 25% were in pivotal studies, and one (Five Prime) had completed Phase II. None was a preclinical platform company.
  • Separately (Bay Bridge Bio, 11 April 2023): "Since 2010, all but one $1bn+ biotech acquisition involved a Phase 3 or approved asset."
  • The conclusions, verbatim: "platforms have underperformed, but you need a platform to build a lasting company"; "the value of a platform decreases with scale" when program quality is low; "Good clinical data is the currency of value in biotech"; "Platform risk is the last risk that biotech investors take over the course of a business cycle."
  • The last line is the one that matters for seed timing: pure-platform funding was "significant in only a few years over the last 25: 2020–2021 and 1999–2000." Both were followed by the cohort's destruction. The 2021 AI-bio seed cohort was funded in the second of exactly two windows in a quarter-century when platform risk was fundable at all.

4. Hypothesis overflow — the verification step became the constraint

The 2026 peer-reviewed review names this directly. Khairil et al. (§9.3) describe "hypothesis overflow," in which hypotheses are generated faster than they can be tested, and state that the binding constraint is now the pace of experimental verification rather than computing capacity. This is the mechanism by which a working model produces no value: the output is not the limiting input. It is the same shape as Jack Scannell's formulation in the group's prior research — "feeding AI with data from poor biological models simply increases the number of wrong answers you can generate per second" — but arrived at from the opposite direction, by authors who are sympathetic to the technology.

5. Data quality, and data that was never generated for modelling

  • Khairil et al. §10.2: "biological and clinical data are often partial, unavailable and skewed"; plus data silos, inconsistent formats, and dimensionality. §8.2.2: a significant portion of healthcare data is fragmented and low quality.
  • Steiner (Timmerman): data generated to answer specific hypotheses differs from data that models need — the industry's historical data is not a training corpus that happens to be badly formatted, it is a different kind of object.
  • Jerel Davis (Versant Ventures), in the Globe and Mail: "It's always garbage in, garbage out."
  • Khairil et al. add the subtlest version: "AI is trained almost exclusively on successful results." The training corpus has the same survivorship bias as the company sample this file exists to correct.

6. The validating side, not the modelling side, was understaffed

  • Dion Madsen, in the Globe and Mail: the hiccups came "not from the technology, but from not having the right quality of people on the validating side." The same article reports a company that "didn't have experienced drug developers on staff to validate predictions in the lab."
  • Clarissa Desjardins (Congruence Therapeutics): "It takes four years just to speak scientific language, let alone understand how to develop a drug."
  • Khairil et al. §10.4: "The lack of alignment between developers of technologies and biologists usually results in" — the sentence is truncated in the fetched text, but the section links this misalignment to overpromising in publications and funding requests.
  • Note the direction of the finding: it is not that AI-bio teams lacked AI talent. Every post-mortem locates the deficit on the biology and development side. This is a hiring-pattern cause with a directly checkable seed-stage signature.

7. Capital intensity meeting a closed exit window

The 2021 cohort's structural problem was that its business model required a decade of capital and the capital market closed in eighteen months.

  • Bay Bridge Bio (April 2023): the IPO market down nearly 90% from 2021 to 2022, cutting expected IPO returns from ~1.5× to ~0.1×; an ~$8bn shortfall of follow-on capital for Series A companies against the prior year; XBI down ~50% from its February 2021 peak; and pharma M&A able to buy only ~10% of each startup — "not enough to return investors' capital."
  • Phase III companies kept most of their value; everything earlier was hit hard. The penalty was applied precisely to the stage the AI cohort occupied.
  • The group's prior research found the same cause operating at the asset level: "the leading cause of death for AI clinical assets is capital, not biology" — more discontinuations from sponsor prioritisation, cost-cutting and insolvency than from missed endpoints.

8. The arithmetic of a single-asset platform

A cause that is structural and rarely stated as a cause. Steiner (Timmerman, January 2024) runs it: at a 12-year average development time and a 10% clinical success rate, even doubling the success rate and halving development time yields only a ~4% net probability of two clinical successes, requiring a minimum of 25 pipeline candidates per platform for the platform thesis to pay off. Almost no AI-native company has ever had 25 candidates in the clinic. Recursion set out to produce 100 clinical candidates in ten years and has five in trials after a decade (Globe and Mail). The platform thesis was arithmetically underpowered at the portfolio sizes these companies could afford — independent of whether the science worked.

What does not recur

Worth stating, because its absence is informative:

  • No post-mortem blames model architecture or compute. Not one of the sources above attributes a failure to having chosen the wrong network or having too few GPUs.
  • Fraud is essentially absent from the drug-discovery failures. The documented integrity failures in this space are in the adjacent scientific literature (§d), not in the companies.
  • Nobody claims the molecules were bad. The Phase I data say the opposite: AI-originated molecules are tolerated at least as well as conventional ones. The field solved the problem it set out to solve and that problem turned out not to be the one that mattered.
  • "Management" appears, but as a conclusion rather than a cause. Sarah Constantin's Zymergen analysis (in the group's prior research) explicitly refuses the culture-clash reading — the staff were good, the work was good — and locates the failure at the "strategic and management level", specifically in optimising a step that was not the bottleneck. That is cause 1 wearing a management label.

(f) What this does to the interpretation of any seed-survivor study

Blunt version, in the order the damage happens.

1. The sample is drawn from the top decile of its cohort, and you cannot see the draw. If ~60% of Series A biotechs never raise a Series B (Bay Bridge Bio, April 2023) and seed→A conversion is lower than that, then a company that is visible today — with a website that resolves, a pipeline page, recent press, a conference talk — has already passed two or three selection gates. Any trait shared by such a sample is a trait of things that survived selection. It is not a specification of what was required at the gate, because the gate rejected companies that had the trait too.

2. Every candidate "requirement" in this literature is observably insufficient. This is the strongest single argument in the file, and it is made entirely by the failures:

Each row is a counterexample to a claim of the form "seed investors require X." At most these are filters investors apply, not predictors of outcome. A survivor study that reports them as filters is reporting the truth; a synthesis that reads them as predictors is making the survivorship error in its strongest form. And the comparison that makes the point hardest is inside a single sub-field: Lycia, Avilar, Firefly Bio and Entact Bio had materially the same thing at seed — a 2020–2024 extracellular protein-modulation platform, a large institutional round, pharma participation in three of four, no clinical asset — and produced an IPO, a stall, a no-trace and a silent bridge respectively.

3. The success-rate evidence has the same bias in miniature. The 80–90% Phase I figure is computed on molecules that reached Phase I. The companies whose candidates never filed an IND are not in the denominator, and §(c) shows that is the larger group. Independent of sample size — and n=24 is already too small to separate from the baseline — the AI success-rate literature measures conditional-on-arrival performance and is routinely read as unconditional.

4. The literature's base rate has the same bias again. Khairil et al.: "AI is trained almost exclusively on successful results." The models, the published benchmarks and the company sample are all conditioned on success. Three independent layers of the same error, compounding.

5. The time-asymmetry makes failure systematically under-weighted at the moment of judgement. A success is announced the day it happens, with a press release and a funding round. A failure is announced never: Avilar's signal is the absence of a Form D; Deep Genomics' is the absence of an NCT number. A 2026 observer counting evidence in the public record will find successes at full strength and failures at a fraction of theirs, not because anyone is lying but because the recording mechanism is one-sided. This is the operational reason survivorship bias in this field is larger than intuition suggests.

6. The one claim that does survive. The failures agree on their cause (§e) far more than the survivors agree on their method. That asymmetry is usable. A study of survivors cannot tell you what to require; the failure record can tell you what to disqualify — a platform whose value depends on a portfolio larger than its capital can fund; a team whose deficit is on the validation side; a thesis whose improvement lands in Phase I; and a company whose data were generated in a system other than the one the claim must hold in. Those are negative tests, they are cheap to run at seed, and every one of them is derived from the companies that are no longer visible.

7. Finally, the honest size of the correction is unknown. Nobody has published a denominator. Searching Europe PMC for analyses of survivorship bias or selective reporting in AI drug discovery specifically returns nothing on point (327 broad hits, none addressing it; checked 11 November 2026). No census of the AI-bio seed cohort of 2020–2022 with an outcome column was found. The correction in this file is therefore directional and worked-example-based, not a computed adjustment. That is itself the most important gap below.
