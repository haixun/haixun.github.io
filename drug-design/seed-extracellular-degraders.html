Evidence file

Seed and first-round evidence: extracellular and targeted protein degraders

An evidence file for the research synthesis on what drug-discovery startups actually hold in hand when they take their first institutional money. Compiled 11 November 2026, covering nineteen companies. Scope: Tier 1, companies that destroy proteins outside the cell by routing them to a lysosome through a cell-surface receptor (ASGPR, CI-M6PR, FcγRIIB, a membrane E3 ligase, a recycling cytokine receptor); Tier 2, the older and better-funded intracellular targeted-protein-degradation branch, included for contrast. Method: SEC EDGAR primaries first and by preference — S-1 and S-1/A registration statements, 424B4 prospectuses, 10-Ks, 10-Qs and every Form D on file, downloaded and read in full — then ClinicalTrials.gov and PubMed/NCBI E-utilities for dates, then domain-registry records, then company press releases; web search was used only where those failed. Research was split across three parallel threads and reconciled here. Every figure is tagged [SEC] (independently verifiable from a filing), [registry], [journal], [GOV], [company-reported] or [press]/[3P]. Where sources conflict I record both rather than choosing. "Not found" means not found, never estimated.

Two structural limits shape what follows. First, the evidence is radically uneven between the tiers. All five of the big Tier 2 companies are public and so had to disclose financing history, licence terms and partnership economics in an S-1; most of Tier 1 is private, and six of the nineteen companies here — Neomorph, Magnet, Vicinitas, Merida, plus Plexium's and Monte Rosa's first rounds — have no checkable filing for their first round at all. Read any Tier 1 versus Tier 2 comparison with that asymmetry in mind. Second, the session's shared web-search budget was exhausted partway through all three threads, which left founder credentials — PhD labs, postdoc hosts, academy memberships — as the weakest field in the file, and the sweep for 2025–26 new entrants incomplete. Both limits are itemised in "Gaps and conflicts," along with the twenty-three source conflicts I could not resolve. Several of the group's working anchors turned out to be wrong — including the claim that EpiBiologics dosed the first extracellular degrader in humans, the size of Lycia's Series A, and Novartis's supposed role in Firefly Bio — and those corrections are called out inline and collected at the end.

The short version

  1. In this segment, the first institutional round is almost never a seed and almost never follows a paper. Of the five Tier 1 companies with a traceable first money date, four were built inside a venture firm (Versant's Inception engine, RA Capital's NewCo group, Third Rock's EIR programme, Versant again for Firefly) and the "Series A" was the firm writing itself a cheque into its own creation. The academic paper, where there was one, published after the money moved.
  2. Lycia's famous "$50 million Series A" was never fully funded. Its March 2020 Form D declares a $50,390,410 offering of which $25,390,410 was sold and $25,000,000 remained [SEC]. The S-1 confirms Series A closed at exactly $25,390,410 — the second tranche was never drawn. The press release said "$50 million commitment"; the cash was half that [SEC vs company-reported].
  3. Lycia's first money (first sale 3 March 2020) predates its own founding paper by five months. Banik et al., Nature, epub 29 July 2020 [journal]. The ASGPR paper that actually underpins the clinical programme (Ahn et al., Nat Chem Biol) epub 25 March 2021 — after Series A, before Series B.
  4. Avilar has raised exactly one round in six years and still calls it a seed. $75,276,164 total, first sale 14 December 2020, closed out by February 2023 [SEC] — launched publicly as a "$60 million seed financing" [company-reported]. Six years on, as of October 2026 it is still preclinical with a new CEO.
  5. The clinical data bar for a big exit is one dose-escalation study. Merida's MER511 Phase 1 started 19 December 2025 [registry]; Lilly agreed to buy the company on 31 August 2026 for up to $2.875bn, closing in October 2026 [SEC]. About eight months of Phase 1.
  6. Lycia's IPO: 17,640,000 shares at $18.00 = $317,520,000 gross, $295,293,600 net before expenses, prospectus dated 22 October 2026 [SEC] — on n≈6 active participants in one dose cohort.
  7. Academic licences in this field are nearly free. Monte Rosa bought a perpetual, fully-paid, worldwide exclusive licence to the Institute of Cancer Research's compound library for CHF 40,000 of shares plus ~$42,000 [SEC]. Yale's whole MoDE platform licence to Biohaven carries $538k of development and $2.95m of commercial milestones remaining; Lycia's Stanford LYTAC licence carries up to $1.9m per product and was not even filed as a material contract in its S-1 [SEC]. Biohaven owes the founder's consulting vehicle (Moda Pharmaceuticals) up to $157.9m — roughly 45× what Yale gets.
  8. $1 billion for a preclinical asset. Johnson & Johnson completed its acquisition of Firefly Bio for $1,000,000,000 in cash on 29 July 2026, as an asset acquisition with a ~$1bn IPR&D charge [SEC] — about 29 months after a Series A announced at $94m (SEC-verifiable: $40.0m sold), with no IND, no registered trial and no named candidate ever disclosed. Its academic co-founder was Carolyn Bertozzi — the same Nobel laureate who founded Lycia.
  9. Failure here is silent, and it is not visible in press coverage. Neither apparent failure was ever announced. Avilar simply stopped filing Form Ds after February 2023. Vicinitas Therapeutics' domain lapsed and was re-registered by a third party on 5 May 2026 [registry]; it never registered a trial and is still listed on a16z's portfolio page.
  10. Six of these nineteen companies have no checkable filing at all. Neomorph, Magnet and Vicinitas have never filed a Form D; Merida has no locatable EDGAR filer; Plexium's and Monte Rosa's first rounds are invisible. Two financings exist only in filings and were never announced: Plexium's $60,115,703 (July 2025) and Triana's $6.0m + $10.0m seed tranches.
  11. Tier 2 (intracellular TPD) started smaller, not larger. First cheques of $3.0m (Kymera), $3.1m (Nurix), $5.0m (Monte Rosa) and $6.0m (Triana) against Tier 1's $20–40m — because Tier 1's venture-creation rounds had to fund the platform validation in-house. The comparison is in "What the pattern shows".

Tier 1 company detail

Lycia Therapeutics — the only one with a full public paper trail

Identity. Incorporated in Delaware on 24 October 2019 as Lycia Therapeutics, Inc.; principal operations in South San Francisco (S-1/A, p. 228 / F-1). 31 full-time employees as of 30 June 2026 [SEC].

How it was built. Versant Ventures "established Lycia in 2019 within the firm's San Diego-based Inception labs in collaboration with academic founder Carolyn Bertozzi" (launch release, 9 June 2020). The initial aim, in the firm's own words, was "to develop and validate a drug discovery platform" — i.e. the company existed to do the de-risking that a licensor would normally have done. Clare Ozawa (Versant MD) and Laurent Fischer joined the board in December 2019 [SEC]; Aetna Wun Trombley became CEO in April 2020 [SEC] — roughly six months after incorporation and one month after the Series A first sale. Versant holds through two vehicles, Versant Vantage II, L.P. (the opportunity/creation fund) and Versant Venture Capital VII, L.P. [SEC].

First institutional round — the correction. The Form D filed 17 March 2020 (acc. 0001806540-20-000001) reports:

  • date of first sale: 3 March 2020
  • total offering amount: $50,390,410
  • total amount sold: $25,390,410
  • total remaining to be sold: $25,000,000

The S-1/A's convertible-preferred table closes the loop: Series A finished at 25,390,410 shares at $1.00 = $25,390,410 aggregate liquidation value [SEC]. The remaining $25m tranche was never called; the company went to Series B instead. The June 2020 press release's "$50 million commitment from founding investor Versant Ventures" is therefore an authorised amount, not cash raised — and the widely repeated "Lycia launched with a $50m Series A" overstates the actual first cheque by 2×.

Related persons on that first Form D: Jeremy S. Caldwell (Executive Officer and Director, listed at 6175 Nancy Ridge Drive, San Diego — the Inception labs address), Clare Ozawa, Thomas Woiwode, Laurent Fischer [SEC]. Trombley is absent, consistent with her April 2020 start.

What existed at the first round — ladder level 2, arguably 1.5. At the 3 March 2020 first sale there was: a Versant-created company five months old, no CEO, a chair-of-SAB arrangement with Bertozzi, and the CI-M6PR science. The launch release four months later cites "a 2019 publication" [company-reported] — but the peer-reviewed paper, Banik SM, Pedram K, Wisnovsky S, Ahn G, … Bertozzi CR, "Lysosome-targeting chimaeras for degradation of extracellular proteins," Nature, PMID 32728216, epub 29 July 2020 [journal] — appeared nearly five months after the money and seven weeks after the launch announcement. The 2019 reference is to the bioRxiv preprint. Record this as a conflict with the group's anchor, which dates the founding paper to Nature 2020: both are right about the journal, but the company was funded off a preprint.

By the June 2020 launch the company had moved to ladder level 4: "Confirmatory studies have shown targeted degradation of cell surface proteins such as EGFR, PD-L1, as well as secreted proteins like ApoE4" [company-reported, no figures disclosed]. Ozawa's quote is explicit that the pipeline did not yet exist: "With this financing, we hope to build on this progress and to generate a broad pipeline of development candidates."

The ASGPR paper that actually underpins the clinical programmes — Ahn G, Banik SM, Miller CL, Riley NM, … "LYTACs that engage the asialoglycoprotein receptor for targeted protein degradation," Nat Chem Biol, PMID 33767387, epub 25 March 2021 [journal] — landed between Series A and Series B. A third Bertozzi-lab paper, Ahn et al., Science 2023 (PMID 37856615), elucidated the cellular determinants of LYTAC-mediated membrane protein degradation.

Licence from the founding lab. Lycia "owns 15 patent families and exclusively licenses one patent family from Stanford University" covering LYTACs [SEC]. The terms are modest and are not even named after Stanford in the notes: under "the LYTAC license agreement" the company "may be required to make aggregate milestone payments of up to $1.9 million upon achievement of certain development and regulatory milestones for each licensed product," plus royalties on future sales; total expense recognised across all "other" licence agreements was $0.1m (2024) and $0.3m (2025) [SEC]. A separate licence carries up to $8.3m of milestones per research plan [SEC].

A telling structural detail: the S-1's exhibit index lists only one licence as a material contract — the RSR Limited agreement (Exhibit 10.8). Neither the Stanford LYTAC licence nor the Lilly collaboration agreement is filed as a material-contract exhibit [SEC]. Lycia did not regard the licence on its founding academic IP as material enough to disclose to investors. That is probably the single cleanest evidence in this file that, in this field, the academic licence is not the asset.

A second, non-exclusive in-licence matters commercially: the RSR Limited agreement of 1 July 2025 for thermostable TSHR patents underpinning LCA-0321 — $0.3m upfront, annual maintenance fees rising from $50,000 (first anniversary) to $0.3m (fifth and after), aggregate milestones up to $12.0m (first $0.3m due on the earlier of the first IND or 31 January 2027), and a mid-single-digit percentage of sublicence income; term runs to the last-to-expire licensed patent, expected 2035 [SEC]. Note that an outside third-party licence for one programme costs Lycia ~6× what the founding Stanford platform licence does.

Pharma partnership and its failure. In August 2021 Lycia signed a research and collaboration agreement with Eli Lilly covering novel degraders against up to five targets, with a $35.0 million non-refundable, non-creditable upfront plus preclinical, development and commercial milestones and royalties. In August 2025 Lilly exercised a one-year extension and paid a $2.0 million extension fee. No milestones were ever achieved. In November 2025 Lilly gave notice of termination without cause, effective 12 February 2026 [SEC]. Collaboration revenue recognised: $14.9m (2024), $6.7m (2025, including acceleration of remaining deferred revenue on termination), $2.3m (H1 2025), zero in H1 2026 [SEC]. The group's anchor is correct on substance; the $35m upfront and the zero-milestones fact are both confirmed in the S-1.

Restructuring. June 2025: corporate reorganisation eliminating approximately 42% of the workforce; FY2025 charges of $739k employee termination benefits, $489k equipment impairment, $38k stock option modification, $120k prepaid write-off, $142k other = $1,528k total; certain research activities deprioritised [SEC]. Clinical stage and the data behind the IPO. Vicinitas Therapeutics — five months from paper to $65m, and then silence

Scope note. Vicinitas is the inverse of the rest of this file: a DUBTAC (deubiquitinase-targeting chimera) stabilises a target protein by recruiting a deubiquitinase to strip its ubiquitin chains, rather than destroying it. It belongs in the comparison set as an induced-proximity company built on the same venture logic, not as an extracellular degrader.

Founding paper, verified [journal]. Henning NJ, Boike L, Spradlin JN, Ward CC, et al. (Nomura DK lab, UC Berkeley). "Deubiquitinase-targeting chimeras for targeted protein stabilization." Nat Chem Biol, PMID 35210618, epub 24 February 2022 — the OTUB1-recruiting DUBTAC concept. The group's anchor ("Henning et al., Nature Chemical Biology 2022") is correct, and the epub date of February 2022 is the relevant one for dating any round against it.

Identity. Spun out of a 2017 Novartis Institutes for BioMedical Research (NIBR) – UC Berkeley alliance; Berkeley / South San Francisco. Incorporation year not found. No SEC filings of any kind — no CIK, no Form D (verified by EDGAR company-name search and full-text search) [SEC], so every financing figure below is company-reported and cannot be checked against a filing.

First institutional round. $65 million Series A announced 28 July 2022, co-led by Andreessen Horowitz (a16z) and Deerfield Management, with Droia Ventures, GV (Alphabet), The Mark Foundation for Cancer Research and the Berkeley Catalyst Fund (a16z announcement; BioPharma Dive; BioWorld) [company-reported]. a16z credits Cam Wheeler of Deerfield with having "helped lay the company's foundation" and George Golumbeski (Celgene alum, Droia) with joining the company-building effort — venture-assembled around a licensed platform rather than founder-led. Date conflict: 28 July 2022 (a16z, BioWorld, Nomura lab) versus 1 August 2022 (Pulse 2.0) and 1 July 2022 (Caplight); 28 July is best supported. No seed, Series B or later round found.

What existed at the first round — ladder level 2, the cleanest "paper as the founding asset" case in the file. The founding asset is a single paper five months old: Henning et al., Nat Chem Biol 2022;18(4):412–421, epub 24 February 2022 (PMID 35210618) [journal]. a16z's own post points to "a recent paper… that reported the first design and use of this bifunctional molecule" and gives no further data [company-reported]. The paper's data are in vitro / cell-based: a covalent OTUB1 recruiter conjugated to a CFTR corrector stabilised CFTR-ΔF508 in cells, and the approach also stabilised WEE1. So the science sat at level 4 — but it was generated in the academic lab and at NIBR, not by the company: no company-generated data, no in vivo efficacy, no development candidate. The author list is itself the evidence of the hybrid structure, with roughly fifteen NIBR/Novartis scientists alongside the Berkeley group. Correction to the research brief. The premise that Firefly launched "with Novartis involvement" is not supported by any source. Firefly was built inside Versant's Ridgeline Discovery Engine at Basel Technology Park and the pharma name on its cap table is Eli Lilly, not Novartis. The only Novartis connection is that co-founder Bernhard Geierstanger previously led ADC teams there. What existed at the first round — ladder level 5, the highest in this file. At launch Firefly reported that in "solid and liquid tumor research, a single dose led to significant reductions in tumor volume at very low doses" [company-reported, no figures disclosed], said it had "established proof-of-concept" for its lead asset at Ridgeline, and had "two co-lead programs advancing toward the clinic." Founders. Carolyn R. Bertozzi, Ph.D. — co-founder, 2022 Nobel Laureate in Chemistry, Stanford, described at launch as "a leading ADC expert"; scientific-only, no operating role [company-reported]. Note the cross-link: Bertozzi is the academic founder of both Lycia and Firefly Bio — the same laureate seeded two companies in this segment, one in 2019 with Versant's Inception engine and one in 2022 with Versant's Ridgeline engine, and held an operating role in neither. Bernhard Geierstanger, Ph.D. — co-founder and Chief Technology Officer; previously led ADC teams at Novartis and Merck; confirmed as an Executive Officer on the Form D [SEC]. Scott Hirsch — CEO (President per the Form D signature); previously Chief Operating Officer of Allakos; an operating CEO recruited from industry, not a founder-scientist.

Modality. Degrader-antibody conjugates (DACs): ADC architecture with a targeted protein degrader as the payload instead of a cytotoxin, joined by the proprietary Firelink™ linker, designed to cut free payload in circulation and limit uptake by healthy cells. Hirsch on the technical problem: degrader payloads "are large, so they are difficult to conjugate, and their hydrophobicity makes them prone to getting sucked up into cells nonspecifically" [company-reported]. Oncology first, immunology named second. At acquisition J&J disclosed the platform targets pan-KRAS and other drivers of hard-to-treat solid tumours [SEC].

Clinical stage: none. No Firefly Bio study was ever registered on ClinicalTrials.gov [registry], and J&J describes the acquired assets as "preclinical candidates" [SEC].

Outcome — SEC-verified. Announced 8 June 2026: a definitive agreement for J&J to acquire Firefly Bio for $1 billion in cash (J&J release). Completed 29 July 2026, confirmed verbatim in J&J's own 8-K exhibit: "Johnson & Johnson today announced the successful completion of its acquisition of Firefly Bio, Inc. … for $1 billion in cash… The transaction will be accounted for as an asset acquisition, resulting in an in-process research and development charge of approximately $1 billion in the third quarter of 2026," with expected dilution to adjusted EPS of ~$0.46 in 2026 and ~$0.08 in 2027 (J&J 8-K EX-99.1, 29 Jul 2026) [SEC]. John Reed, M.D., Ph.D. (EVP, Innovative Medicine R&D) framed the rationale as KRAS: "KRAS has notoriously been considered an undruggable target."

The return math, and why it matters to this file. Against $94m of announced equity — of which only $40.0m is SEC-verifiable as sold — Firefly returned $1.0 billion in all cash, roughly 29 months after its Series A was announced, with nothing in the clinic. No IND, no trial registration, no named candidate ever made public. Firefly's domain no longer resolves. This is the only exit in either tier achieved without a single human dose, and it is the sharpest counterexample to the otherwise clean rule that the first human biomarker readout is what clears the market. The variable that substituted for clinical data was modality scarcity: a working linker for degrader payloads, in a year when every large pharma wanted a pan-KRAS play.

What the pattern shows about this segment

1. "Seed" is a misnomer; the unit of account is the venture-created, tranched A round. Across Tier 1 there is exactly one classically-sized seed — Aliada's $1,000,000 in June 2021 [SEC] — and it belongs to the company that isn't really a degrader. Every other first round is a venture firm funding a company it built itself: Versant's Inception engine (Lycia), RA Capital's NewCo group (Avilar), Third Rock's EIR programme (Merida), Versant's Discovery Engine again (Firefly). The academic founder, where present, supplies IP and an SAB seat, not a company.

A group modelling "how much do you need to start" should use the first-tranche number — $6m to $42m across both tiers, $20m to $40m in Tier 1 — not the press release.

2a. And for a third of these companies there is no filing to check at all. Neomorph, Magnet Biomedicine and Vicinitas Therapeutics have never filed a Form D [SEC]; Merida has no locatable EDGAR filer; Plexium's and Monte Rosa's first rounds are invisible (Plexium filed nothing until 2025; Monte Rosa's Series A was issued by its Swiss AG and so generated no US Form D). For those six the round sizes — $109m, $50m, $65m, $121m, $28m and $19.25m — rest entirely on company press releases. Conversely, two financings exist only in the filings and were never announced: Plexium's $60,115,703 round of July 2025 and Triana's two seed tranches of $6.0m and $10.0m. Anyone reconstructing this sector from press coverage will overstate the announced rounds and miss the unannounced ones.

3. The money reliably arrives before the paper. Three of three cases where both dates are knowable:

Company First sale Founding paper (epub) Gap
Lycia 3 Mar 2020 Banik, Nature, 29 Jul 2020 money 5 months early
Avilar 14 Dec 2020 (no licensed paper; Ahn ASGPR paper 25 Mar 2021) money 3 months early, and not its paper

The inference is that the asset being financed is not the publication — it is the lab's private, pre-publication knowledge plus the venture firm's willingness to pay for the validation work. The ladder level at first money is therefore 1–2 in every Tier 1 case, and the climb to level 4–5 happens inside the company, on the first round's money, before public launch. Avilar's launch (level 5, in vivo rodent and NHP data, eleven months after first money) and Lycia's (level 4, in vitro EGFR/PD-L1/ApoE4, fifteen months after) are both the end of the first round's work, not the beginning.

The pattern is stark: the step change in valuation comes from the first human pharmacodynamic readout, not from the platform. Lycia went from a flat insider round to a $1.3–1.65bn public market capitalisation on one cohort; Lilly paid up to $2.875bn for Merida eight months after first-in-human dosing; Biohaven extracted an $80m upfront with ~20% royalties from Ono on a Phase-3-ready IgG degrader. Conversely, Avilar — strong in vivo data, two platforms, a granted patent — has raised nothing new in nearly four years. In extracellular degradation, animal data does not clear the market; the first human biomarker does. That is unusually clean because the whole class has a serum biomarker (IgE, IgG, Gd-IgA1, TSHR autoantibody titre) readable in a handful of healthy volunteers within days. The segment's capital efficiency is a direct consequence of its biomarker, not of its biology.

4a. The one exception proves what substitutes for clinical data: modality scarcity. Firefly Bio returned $1.0 billion in cash, 29 months after its Series A, with no IND, no registered trial and no named candidate ever disclosed [SEC]. It is the only exit in either tier achieved without a single human dose. What it had instead was a working linker for degrader payloads at the moment every large pharma wanted a pan-KRAS play. Note the symmetry with the rule rather than against it: Firefly was also the only company in the file at ladder level 5 at its first round — in vivo tumour-volume reductions, generated inside Versant's Ridgeline engine before the round was announced. It never needed a human readout because it never had to raise again.

4b. Failure in this segment is silent. Neither of the two apparent failures was ever announced. Avilar simply stopped filing Form Ds after February 2023, changed CEO in October 2026 and remains preclinical. Vicinitas let its domain lapse — vicinitastx.com was re-registered by a Chinese registrant on 5 May 2026 [registry] — never registered a trial, never issued a press release after launch, and is still listed on its lead investor's public portfolio page. There is no shutdown notice, no asset sale, no 8-K. For a group building a base rate of outcomes, this matters more than any single success: the denominator is not observable from press coverage, and a portfolio page is not evidence that a company is alive.

Notes on evidence

What is strong here. Every financing figure for the five public Tier 2 companies, for Lycia and for Biohaven is traceable to a filing that carries legal liability — an S-1 equity footnote, a 424B4 cover page, a 10-K licence note or a Form D. The same is true of all five exits: Lilly's 10-Q on Merida, AbbVie's 10-K on Aliada, J&J's 8-K exhibit on Firefly, Lycia's 424B4 on its own IPO, and Biohaven's 10-Q on the Ono terms. Licence economics are the single best-evidenced field in the file, because public companies must disclose them and private ones need not. Publication dates all come from NCBI records rather than from company claims about their own science, which is what makes the "money before the paper" finding checkable.

What is weak here, and why. Three things, in order of how much they should temper the conclusions:

  1. Private-company round sizes. Form D reports an offering and the amount sold as of the filing date; it is not a final round total, and amendments lag. Where a company never amended, the figure is a floor. And for six companies there is no filing at all, so $109m (Neomorph), $121m (Merida), $65m (Vicinitas), $50m (Magnet), $28m (Plexium) and $19.25m (Monte Rosa's Series A) rest entirely on press releases that no regulator reviewed.
  2. Founder credentials. This is the field the exhausted search budget damaged most. Institutional profile pages (Yale for Crews, UCSF for Wells, HHMI for Rapé and Weiss) gave solid answers where I could reach them; company bio pages are company-reported and, in Plexium's case, demonstrably mis-keyed. I have written "not found" for roughly thirty specific credentials rather than reaching for what I half-remembered — including several, like Crews's NAS status and Bradner's company foundings, that are probably true and widely repeated. Do not fill those in from memory when drafting the synthesis.
  3. Every clinical percentage in this file. All of them are company-reported, from open-label, single-arm or partially unblinded early studies with single-digit to low-double-digit numbers of participants on active drug. Lycia's headline 96% rests on roughly six active participants in one cohort with acknowledged missing samples; Monte Rosa's 100% PSA response rate rests on five patients in a biomarker-selected subgroup of a fifteen-patient trial with no comparator arm; Biohaven's, Nurix's and C4T's figures carry the same caveats. None has been peer-reviewed or presented with full statistical treatment, and several appear only in prospectuses alongside the issuer's own warning that they "may differ from future results of the same studies." These numbers should never be compared across companies as though they were trial results. Their role in this file is to mark when a company first had a human readout, not how good the drug is.

One methodological finding worth carrying into the synthesis. The gap between what companies announce and what they file is not noise — it is systematic, it runs about 2× in the same direction every time, and it is only visible if you read Form Ds. Any sector map built from press coverage will overstate first rounds, miss unannounced ones entirely (Plexium's $60.1m, Triana's $16m of seed), and show no failures at all, because failure in this segment is never announced.
