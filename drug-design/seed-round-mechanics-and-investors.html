Evidence file

Seed round mechanics and the investor landscape in AI-for-drug-discovery

An evidence file, not an essay. Compiled 11 November 2026 for the ai4bio exploration, to feed a synthesis document on how first rounds in this sector actually work: how much, from whom, on what terms. Method: primary sources where reachable (firm websites, law-firm quarterly deal-terms reports, agency program pages, company counsel announcements), third-party aggregates where not, and — for the amount distributions — my own computation from a company-level deal list rather than any aggregator's reported average. Every figure is labelled with its source, and with whether it is a median or a mean. Limits, stated up front because they are severe: Carta blocked every request (HTTP 403) from this session, so every Carta figure here is second-hand through blogs that disagree with each other; the PitchBook-NVCA PDF would not parse; the HSBC Innovation Banking detail tables live in PDFs I could not reach; I have no year-by-year AI-bio seed data before 2024; and the session's web-search budget was exhausted partway through, so several planned lines of enquiry (academic translational funds, the traditional preclinical-duration baseline, pharma venture arms' stated criteria) are marked not researched rather than not found. "Not found" means not found. Nothing here is estimated.

The short version

  1. The central structural fact: classic biotech often has no seed round to compete for, because the seed-stage work happens inside the venture firm before the company exists. 5AM Ventures says this almost explicitly on its own site: its 4:59 Initiative is a "hands-on internal effort to discover, incubate, and fund breakthrough science" whose purpose is to generate early data for a Series A, and its venture strategy is "early-stage focused, with an emphasis on Series A companies." The word seed does not appear. Third Rock says it spends a long period "at the seed stage, validating mechanisms and maturing a lead program well before a company launches." Versant runs "in-house teams of multi-disciplinary scientists working in state-of-the-art laboratory facilities" that "enable the launch of novel biotechnology companies." The Column Group is "primarily focused on company formation." In each case the seed is an internal cost centre, not a financing event.
  2. But the label is not the mechanism, and 2025–26 broke the label. Flagship Pioneering launched Lila Sciences in March 2025 with $200M of what it and the trade press called "seed" capital. Xaira launched in April 2024 with ">$1bn in committed capital" — the company's own phrase — which one tracker records as a Series A and no one records as a seed. A $200M "seed" and a $1bn unlabelled first round are the same mechanism as a Series A; only the vocabulary moved.
  3. The AI-native pattern is real, numerous and much smaller. A hand-curated tracker of AI-drug-discovery financings records 57 seed rounds between 1 January 2024 and 21 September 2026 in this one niche. My computation from that company-level list: pooled median seed $7.0M, pooled mean $11.34M (n=56 disclosed), p10 $1.97M, p90 $28.06M. That is a real ladder, not an artefact: Antiverse, Converge Bio, Orbis Medicines, Chai Discovery and Xellar all raised a seed and then a Series A inside the window.
  4. The field is bifurcating, not converging. Model and tool companies run the software ladder with software investors and software instruments — Boltz PBC took a $28M seed from Amplify, a16z and Zetta; Topos Bio $10.5M from Boldstart and Threshold; 10x Science $4.8M from Initialized. Therapeutics companies that need wet labs and a pipeline are still formed and financed the biotech way: Flagship's Expedition Medicines entered at a $50M Series A (Oct 2025), not a seed.
  5. Tranching is the terms difference that survives scrutiny, and Cooley only reports it for one side. Cooley's quarterly venture financing reports publish the share of life sciences rounds structured in tranches — 25.8% (Q4 2024), 27.1% (Q1 2025), 33.3% (Q2 2025, "the highest percentage seen since Q3 2023"), 28.3% (Q3 2025), 23.6% (Q4 2025), 28.1% (Q1 2026), 29.6% (Q2 2026) — and publish no comparable figure for technology. Roughly one life-sciences round in four to one in three is milestone-gated. Note Cooley silently revised two of its own quarters.
  6. Second seeds are at least as common as observed graduations. Of the 56 seed-labelled rounds, 6 companies raised a second round still labelled seed (Graph Therapeutics, Sable Bio, Helical, Manas AI, Relation Therapeutics, Meddenovo) against 5 that reached a Series A inside the window. Relation Therapeutics' second seed was smaller than its first ($35M in Mar 2024, then $26M in Dec 2025, from the same lead). Meddenovo's second was $450K after $1.1M.
  7. Mean-versus-median is not a pedantic caveat here; it is the whole story. AI-drug-discovery Series A 2024: median $32.5M, mean $108.67M — a 3.34x divergence, because Xaira's single round was 70.8% of the year's Series A dollars. Remove one deal and the mean falls from $108.7M to $34.4M while the median barely moves ($32.5M → $29.2M). In 2025 Isomorphic's $600M was 45.3% of the year's Series A dollars. Any sentence in this sector containing an average and no median should be discarded.
  8. AI-bio seeds are roughly 2x general software seeds, but the comparison is soft. AI-drug-discovery seed median pooled 2024–26: $7.0M (my computation, n=56). All-US all-sector median seed deal 2026: $3.0M, down from $3.4M in 2025 — seed was the only stage whose median fell (PitchBook-NVCA Venture Monitor Q3 2026). Ratio ~2.3x. Against the $4.1M Carta figure the ratio would be ~1.7x. Different populations, different windows, different definitions of "seed": the direction is probably right, the magnitude is not reliable.
  9. The famous generalist crossover funds are mostly absent from AI-bio seeds. Of the names the group asked about, only a16z (Boltz) and General Catalyst (Manas AI, Lila) appear as seed investors in the 56-deal set. Lux Capital, Founders Fund, Khosla Ventures, Felicis, Compound, NFX and Civilization Ventures do not appear at all. The repeat seed leads are different names: DCVC (two of the six largest seeds in the dataset), Dimension (Achira $33M, Chai ~$30M), Madrona, redalpine, daphni.
  10. The most active strategic investor at AI-bio seed is a chip company, not a pharma company. NVIDIA's NVentures backed Relation Therapeutics' seed twice and EvolutionaryScale's $142M seed. Across the entire 56-deal seed set I found no seed round from Pfizer Ventures, JJDC, Amgen, AbbVie, Roche, BMS, M Ventures, Boehringer, Sanofi Ventures or GV. Novartis Venture Fund's own site (>$750M committed, 40+ companies) does not mention seed or company creation at all.
  11. Y Combinator applies identical terms to a therapeutics company and a SaaS company, and says so by omission. $500,000 on two post-money SAFEs — $125,000 for 7% plus $375,000 uncapped with MFN — no fees, "not contingent on milestones," pro rata included. YC's deal page does not mention bio or hard tech anywhere. That is the software pattern applied to bio without modification, and it is the exact inverse of the 23.6–33.3% of life-sciences rounds that Cooley finds tranched.
  12. The non-dilutive on-ramp is real and specific. NIH SBIR/STTR Phase I $335,937 and Phase II $2,239,578 (guidelines, not caps). SOSV takes up to $550,000 at pre-seed but has "strict ownership requirements" and needs "more than 10% ownership" for that cheque. Nucleate's Activator "forms pioneering biotech companies without taking equity." Wellcome Leap funds at the program level, $50–60M each, not per company.
  13. There is no biotech-versus-software seed graduation rate. I looked hard and it does not exist in reachable form. The best quantified sector statement I could reach is J.P. Morgan, relayed second-hand, putting seed→A between "1 in 10" and "1 in 3" depending on sector and market conditions — a range, not a biotech figure. Carta's cohort series (30.6% for Q1 2018, ~40% for Q2 2020, 15.4% for Q1 2022, all 2-year windows) is all second-hand and the relays do not reconcile with each other.
  14. The 2026 cohort is being set up for the hardest Series A yet. PitchBook-NVCA counts 8,625 first-time financings through Q3 2026, already above the 8,507 full-year 2021 record, while the median seed round shrank to $3.0M and AI took 82.7% of all US venture dollars. More companies, smaller cheques, and — on the Carta relays — a lower graduation rate than any prior cohort.

(a) How biotech first rounds actually work, versus software

The venture-creation model, in the firms' own words

This is the structural point the group may not know, and it is worth establishing from primary sources rather than from the secondary blogs that dominate search results on it. The claim is not that biotech firms never use the word seed. The claim is that in the venture-creation model the seed-stage work — target validation, mechanism de-risking, lead maturation, team assembly — is performed inside the venture firm, funded off the firm's own balance sheet, before a company exists as a financeable entity. The company's first external financing is therefore a Series A, or an unlabelled block of committed capital.

What Flagship and Xaira actually did, which is the more useful evidence

Two 2024–25 events show that the vocabulary has detached from the mechanism:

  • Lila Sciences launched out of Flagship's labs in March 2025 with $200M that BioPharma Dive and the company both call "seed funding" / "seed capital" — Flagship plus eight other investors, among them General Catalyst, ARK Venture Fund and Altitude Life Science Ventures. Lila is run by a Flagship general partner (Geoff von Maltzahn) with a Flagship senior fellow as CTO (Andrew Beam) and a Flagship origination partner as president of future science (Molly Gibson). It "will not make its own therapeutic candidates" and will instead partner with other Flagship startups and outside biotechs. The article does not explain the sizing or structure of the round. A $200M first round staffed by the sponsoring firm's own partners is the venture-creation model wearing a seed label.
  • Xaira Therapeutics launched 23 April 2024 after roughly six months in stealth with "more than $1 billion in committed capital" — the company's own phrase, not a round name — co-led by ARCH Venture Partners and Foresite Capital, with F-Prime, NEA, Sequoia, Lux, Lightspeed, Menlo Ventures, Two Sigma Ventures and SV Angel. One explainer notes that committed capital "is not the same as a $1 billion valuation or a single $1 billion funding round." The AI-drug-discovery tracker records it as a Series A of >$1B; nobody records it as a seed. The tracker's own methodology says committed capital "is not treated as cash received at close." Two aggregators would give this event two different stage labels, and the company gave it none.

By contrast, Flagship's Expedition Medicines raised a $50M Series A from Flagship Pioneering in October 2025. So the same firm, in the same eighteen months, labelled one creation a $200M seed and another a $50M Series A. The label carries no information about the mechanism.

The AI-drug-discovery tracker records 57 seed rounds and 46 Series A rounds between 1 January 2024 and 21 September 2026 in this single niche, with no round labelled pre-seed at all. Evidence of persistence of the biotech pattern: Cooley's all-stage median deal size was higher in life sciences than in technology in Q1 2026 ($22.2M vs $19.3M); life-sciences rounds are tranched at 23.6–33.3% while Cooley publishes no tranche figure for technology at all; venture-creation firms are still entering at Series A (Expedition Medicines $50M) or at unlabelled nine- and ten-figure first rounds (Xaira, Lila); and the therapeutics end of the 2025 Series A list is dominated by specialist and strategic syndicates (AI Proteins $41.5M from Mission BioCapital and Santé; PsiThera $47.5M from Samsara Biocapital and Lightstone; Syneron Bio ~$100M from AstraZeneca and the AZ-CICC fund).

My reading of the two together: this is a bifurcation along the wet-lab line, not a convergence. Companies selling models, tools and prediction (Boltz, Tamarind, Chai, Topos, Converge, 10x Science, deepmirror) run the software ladder. Companies that intend to own molecules and need laboratories, chemistry and a pipeline are still created and capitalised the biotech way, whatever the first round is called. The brief asked for evidence either way; this is the shape of it.

SAFEs versus priced rounds

Honest answer: I could not find authoritative data on instrument mix split by sector, and the group should not be given a number. What I can establish:

  • Priced rounds dominate wherever a tranche exists, by construction. A milestone-gated tranche, a board seat and a liquidation preference cannot be papered in a SAFE. Cooley's 23.6–33.3% life-sciences tranche rate therefore sets a floor on the share of institutional life-sciences rounds that are priced. This is my inference from Cooley's data, not a claim any source makes.
  • Cooley's life-sciences dataset is a priced-round dataset. Its terms tables report 1x liquidation preference in 95.8–98.2% of deals and nonparticipating preferred stock in ~96% across 2025–26. These are concepts that exist only in priced preferred financings.
  • At the pre-seed boundary, the software instrument is used unmodified. Y Combinator invests $500,000 on two post-money SAFEs — $125,000 for 7% (converting into 7% including any existing option pool) plus $375,000 on an uncapped MFN SAFE (converting at the lowest cap issued between the MFN start date and the priced round; at a $15M post-money cap, 2.5%) — with no fees, "not contingent on milestones," and pro rata rights. YC's deal page does not mention bio or hard tech anywhere. Identical terms for a therapeutics company and a SaaS company.
  • SOSV takes equity, not a note, and is explicit about ownership. Up to $550,000 at pre-seed, with "strict ownership requirements" and a need to reach "more than 10% ownership" for that cheque size. It accepts "angel notes and non-dilutive grants" as prior capital but will not join a company that has already raised a VC-backed seed or Series A.
  • Conflicting third-party claims on note usage, reported because they conflict. One law-oriented newsletter puts convertible notes at "at least 30% of pre-seed funding in Medical Devices, Pharma/Biotech, and Energy." A different vendor's pre-seed market-share table puts notes at "~5–7% overall," concentrated in biotech, medical devices and energy. These cannot both be right. Neither is verified; do not use either.
  • Several vendor guides assert that biotech seeds move to priced equity "once the round includes a dedicated life-sciences lead, because tranches, board seats, and milestone gates are easier to paper in priced rounds," and that SAFEs survive in "pre-seed angel rounds, academic carve-outs, and bridge instruments." This is consistent with everything above but it is vendor-blog reasoning, not data, and I flag it as such.

(b) Amounts, with real numbers

Methodology note, which matters more than usual here

The tables below separate three kinds of figure, and the group should not mix them:

  1. My own computation from a company-level deal list. The AI-drug-discovery tracker at newmarketpitch.com publishes every individual financing it records, with company, amount, date and disclosed investors, plus a stated methodology (private-company equity only; AI had to be core to the business; debt, grants, loans and public offerings excluded; committed capital not treated as cash at close; extensions and tranches reviewed case by case). The tracker does not itself publish stage-level medians. I extracted its 57 seed and 46 Series A rows and computed the distributions directly. This is a third-party aggregate as to its inputs and mine as to its statistics. Known weaknesses: a hand-curated list of ~100 rounds will systematically miss small undisclosed rounds, which biases the medians upward; 10 of the 57 seed rows are approximations or lower bounds (marked ≈ or >); one seed row (AIMEDORIG, Nov 2024) has no disclosed amount and is excluded, giving n=56.

  3. Sector aggregates from HSBC Innovation Banking and the tracker's own period summaries, which are mostly qualitative or mean-only.

What explains the spread

The seed label in this sector spans $450K to $200M — a factor of 440 — and $450K to $80M within the tracker's stricter AI-drug-discovery scope, a factor of 178. Four correlates are visible in the data itself:

  1. What the company is. The right tail is model and platform companies with frontier compute bills: EvolutionaryScale $142M, Radical Numerics $50M, Chai $30M, Boltz $28M. The left tail is software-only tools and small spinouts: deepmirror $2.4M, AI|ffinity $453K, Meddenovo $450K–1.1M, OutSee $2.5M. The binding input at the top of the distribution is GPUs and researchers, not laboratories.
  2. Who formed it. Venture-creation output sits at the top of the distribution regardless of label: Flagship's Lila at $200M ("seed") and Expedition Medicines at $50M ("Series A"); ARCH and Foresite's Xaira at >$1bn ("committed capital").
  3. Founder provenance, priced directly. Chai Discovery raised ~$30M at a $150M valuation at six months old. Radical Numerics' founders co-created Evo and Evo2. EvolutionaryScale's came out of Meta's ESM team. Manas AI has Reid Hoffman as co-founder. Xaira has a former Stanford president. In each case the round was priced off the team before there was anything to measure.
  4. Geography. The sub-$5M band is dominated by UK, European, Indian and CEE names (Sable Bio, deepmirror, Antiverse, AI|ffinity, Kiin Bio, ALP Bio, OutSee, and at Series A Pharmacelera, Peptris, immunitoAI). The $20M+ band is almost entirely US and Chinese. The same scientific claim prices very differently on either side of the Atlantic.

A fifth driver I cannot evidence from this dataset: wet-lab capex. It is the obvious explanation for why a therapeutics company needs more seed money than a tools company, and it is what the venture-creation model exists to absorb, but the tracker does not record whether a company runs its own laboratory and I found no source that codes it. Not found — flagging it as a plausible mechanism, not a measured one.

What I can compute, with its limits stated

Of the 56 seed-labelled AI-drug-discovery rounds between January 2024 and September 2026, I can identify inside the same window:

  • 5 companies that reached a Series A: Orbis Medicines, Chai Discovery, Converge Bio, Xellar Biosystems, Antiverse

This is emphatically not a graduation rate and must not be presented as one. The window is far too short — a company seeded in mid-2026 has had no time at all; the 2024 cohort has had at most 32 months; the tracker captures only disclosed rounds, so quiet graduations and quiet deaths are both invisible; and the tracker's seed and Series A lists are separately curated, so a company could have graduated without both rounds making the list. The one defensible observation is comparative: inside this window, second seeds are at least as common as observed graduations. That is consistent with Incisive's figure of nearly 40% of late-2024 seed/A financings being insider bridges or extensions, and with Cooley's 23.6–33.3% tranche rate. The AI-bio seed is frequently not a single event.

The cohort being created now

PitchBook-NVCA's Q3 2026 data puts three facts together that the synthesis document should not separate:

  • 8,625 first-time financings through Q3 2026, already above the 8,507 full-year 2021 record
  • The median seed deal fell to $3.0M from $3.4M — the only stage whose median declined
  • AI took 82.7% of all US venture dollars, and rounds ≥$100M were ~5% of Q3 deals but 75% of Q3 deal value

More companies are being started than ever, on smaller cheques than last year, into a market where the capital is concentrating at the top of the stack — and, on the Carta relays, into a lower graduation rate than any prior cohort. HSBC's independent read on biopharma points the same way for 1H 2026: investors moved "back toward pre-clinical deals with smaller syndicates and check sizes," while first-financing dollars rose only "because of two outsized mega rounds."
