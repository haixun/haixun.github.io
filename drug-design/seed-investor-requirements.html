Evidence file

Seed investor requirements in AI drug discovery: what investors say, in their own words

An evidence file for the ai4bio/ synthesis. Compiled 11 November 2026. It collects what venture investors, company-creation firms and programme officers have published about the bar for seed funding in biotech and techbio, with short attributed quotations and links, rather than reconstructing the bar from company-by-company funding records (other fronts do that). Method: primary sources first — partner essays, firm "approach" pages, published interviews, published funder guidance — fetched directly and, wherever a quotation appears below without a stated caveat, checked against the raw served page text rather than against a summary. Sections 3d–3h and 6 were gathered by two parallel research efforts; their quotations carry the confidence markers those efforts reported, and the highest-value ones among them were independently re-verified here (see the note at the end of Gaps and conflicts, which states exactly which). Limits: three important constraints. (1) Venture firms publish to attract deal flow, so most of this is marketing as well as information; sections are labelled accordingly. (2) Very little of the published investor writing is specifically about AI drug discovery at seed — most of it is about biotech seed generally, and the AI-specific material is mostly scepticism rather than criticism-free requirement lists. (3) Flagship Pioneering's own site blocked automated access, and several firms (Dimension, Compound, Lux, Khosla, Founders Fund, Civilization) publish via JavaScript-rendered pages that could not be read; their positions are marked "not found" rather than guessed. The session's web-search budget was exhausted partway through, so later work proceeded by direct fetching of known and linked URLs. Every "not found" below is literal.

The short version

  1. No investor publishes a data threshold for seed in biotech, and at least one says so explicitly. Aimee Raleigh (Principal, Atlas Venture) writes that "Sometimes people are disappointed to learn there is no official checklist investors use during a diligence" (LifeSciVC, April 2024). The closest thing to a stated threshold anywhere in this file is her parenthetical that seed deals are expected to fund "preclinical PoC in a relevant model" (July 2025).
  2. The most explicit published seed→Series A ladder comes from NFX, not from a biotech firm. NFX describes pre-seed as "just the scientist with the idea" and seed as the round that buys "the proof point for the Series A round" (January 2021).
  3. The platform/asset pendulum is publicly acknowledged by the investors themselves, and dated. Bruce Booth (Partner, Atlas Venture) opened an October 2024 post: "Today's market likes products. Platforms aren't in vogue anymore." He frames it as the third such "asset-in, platforms-out" swing in 25 years and says "there's a point where the pendulum between assets and platforms has swung too far, and we might be reaching it" (October 2024).
  4. Quantitatively, the only published platform:asset funding ratio found is a single guest commentary. Uciane Scarlett put the "steady state" at "2:1 globally or 3:1 in the U.S.", with a platform peak of 4:1 in 2020–22 (BioCentury, 13 January 2025). This is one source and its global/US labelling is inconsistent; see Gaps and conflicts.
  5. The "load-bearing AI" question is answered mostly by absence. Atlas's own published diligence framework for a new therapeutic thesis — genetics, pharmacology/PK-PD, path to clinical proof of concept, product thesis — never mentions AI, machine learning or computation at all. That is a finding about what counts as evidence, not an oversight to be glossed.
  6. The most explicit investor statement on AI being load-bearing is negative. Booth, October 2026: "I've yet to see repeatable, scalable examples of generative AI finding totally new scaffolds for novel drugs against conventionally undruggable targets", and "Just look at the pipelines of most of the major 'AI Native' discovery shops who claim to be using transformative AI models for drug discovery: they are mostly populated with well-credentialled targets and lots of pre-existing chemotypes or protein sequences" (LifeSciVC, 30 October 2026).
  7. The fee-for-service critique is inverted by techbio investors. NFX presents CRO-like revenue as an advantage: "They have these unique capabilities that other companies can pay for, or collaborate with – similar to a CRO model" (June 2022). Biotech-side analysis treats partnership revenue as value-neutral: of CRISPR Therapeutics' platform partnerships, Bay Bridge Bio notes "none of these moved the stock" (undated).
  8. The nearest thing to a named-investor recalibration is carefully hedged. Peter Hebert, Managing Partner at Lux Capital — an early AI-bio backer — told PitchBook on 9 January 2026: "Investor expectations might have been too large", and that he has seen "some of his software-focused peers retreat back to more familiar pastures". No investor was found saying in their own words that they were wrong about AI drug discovery (PitchBook, 9 January 2026).
  9. No AI-washing enforcement action anywhere touches life sciences. Across the verified SEC and DOJ actions (2024 to early 2025), the sectors are investment advisory, HR tech, restaurant tech and consumer e-commerce. No regulator statement uses "AI-washing" about drug discovery, and no securities class action against an AI drug discovery company over its AI claims was found. See §3g.
  10. The best-stated test for whether AI is load-bearing comes from a journal Perspective, not a venture firm. David Shaywitz, co-author of Bender et al.: "Does it change which experiment gets run, which compound advances, or which weak program gets killed?" (Timmerman Report, 14 August 2026).
  11. The "proprietary data factory is a moat" thesis is contested in public, by name. LongeVC says an investable AI biotech has "datasets that competitors can't easily replicate" (May 2026); Shaywitz, citing Jack Scannell, argues the opposite — a better assay or validated biomarker "may benefit competitors" and is "harder for the organization that paid to create it to monetize" (August 2026). See §3e and §3f.
  12. Every non-dilutive funder describes its exit as reaching investors, not replacing them — NIH ("leverage funding to attract investors and partners"), Harvard Blavatnik ("where an industry partnership can commence"), Breakout Labs ("transition from early start-up to venture-backed company"). The only investor on record about the accelerator path for drug discovery, Bruce Booth in 2014, said the valuation trajectory "won't happen on only Y Combinator's $120K invested into drug discovery stage biotechs."
  13. A widely circulating 2026 quotation on the asset turn is misattributed in practice. "Today the money wants an asset: a specific drug, a specific disease, a clear path to human data" is attributed by its source to Harsha Moole, MD, Founding and Managing Partner at PhysicianEstate — not a biotech venture firm (Cure, 17 August 2026). Flagged so the synthesis does not repeat it as a biotech-VC view.

1. Investor statements on the seed bar in bio and techbio

1a. Techbio-specific seed funds

NFX (Omri Drory, General Partner; with Emily Leproust and James Currier), "Launching NFX Bio and The Rise of the Scientist Founder", published 28 January 2021 (page metadata; last modified 23 May 2023). This is the single most explicit published statement of a stage-by-stage bar found anywhere in this file. It is also unambiguously marketing: the post is a fund launch announcement and ends with a pitch form.

"We're investing in all stages of seed. Pre-seed, when it's just the scientist with the idea. Seed, when the entire team is there and they need some help, maybe to get the IP out and get the proof point for the Series A round. We even do early A's."

"But to really get your IP out there to see if it actually works, to get the proof of concept, you need a few million dollars. And it's hard to find."

"You want to start a company ideally after you peer-reviewed your research and you know that the basic biology works before you go around and try to develop it."

"Obviously, the more proof points you have, the easier it is."

On what they screen for, three criteria — "The market, the technology, and the people":

"One, is it big enough? Is the market big enough? Second, do you have any magic? Is this magic defensible? Is your technology really magical and helping people? Third, are you the right team to execute this magic technology on this huge market to make something special?"

On the kind of company:

"We like to invest in teams with a core technology, especially a core biological technology. And one where that IP allows them to create many applications, not just one small molecule for one application. We want something that helps you create a lot of different diagnostics, a lot of different therapies."

On process (useful as a statement of what is not required — data):

"Nobody's going to invest in a one-pager." … "You cannot cold call an investor. You need to find a way to get a warm introduction."

Source: nfx.com/post/launching-nfx-bio

NFX (Omri Drory, General Partner), "How to Prove Your TechBio Platform at Seed Stage", December 2022. This is the closest thing to a published method for getting from seed to Series A as a platform company. Its central concept is the "minimum platform proof point" (MPPP).

"De-risk your tech before focusing on capturing the largest possible market."

"Be greedy and build up your war chest of clinical results that indicate that your platform is working in the real world. This is mission critical in the first 12-18 months of your company. It will be the strongest signal of traction when you go to raise your next round."

On what investors are looking for in a platform — note this is a direct statement that papers and IP are not sufficient:

"It's an attractor for investors, who will look for evidence that a platform is more than a collection of smart people, peer reviewed papers, and IP."

On the downside risk of choosing a big first indication:

"If you choose an indication that has a larger market, say Alzheimer's, but much higher risk, and your initial pre-clinical trials don't work, you will struggle to raise the next round."

Source: nfx.com/post/prove-techbio-at-seed-stage

NFX (Omri Drory), "From Biotech to TechBio", June 2022. On the shape of a fundable techbio company:

"We like to see companies that are a combination of a vertical platform, and horizontal product approach."

"companies with multiple shots on goal, and multiple ways to get to market."

Source: nfx.com/post/biotech-to-techbio

Pillar VC / Petri (programme page, footer dated © Pillar VC 2022; accessed 11 November 2026). Petri is Pillar's pre-seed bio programme.

"Petri develops pre-seed companies attacking the world's largest problems at the frontier of biology and engineering."

"We believe the future of biotech is founder-led"

The page lists what it provides — "Pre-seed capital and access to a vast network of investors", "Early access and introductions to business development and corporate venture capital groups" — but states no data or milestone requirement for entry. Tony Kulesa's individual published writing on the seed bar: not found within the pages reachable in this session.

Source: pillar.vc/petri

1c. Classic biotech venture-creation firms

Atlas Venture — Aimee Raleigh, Principal, "Best Practices for Pitching, Geared Towards First-Time Founders", LifeSciVC, July 2025. The most useful recent primary statement of what a seed pitch has to carry, and the only explicit named milestone-per-round in this file.

"Ask for enough capital. Often I see pitches funding just barely through an inflection point (e.g., preclinical PoC in a relevant model for seed deals, Ph1b or Ph2a data in a Series A). Budgets and timelines expand 95%+ of the time – do yourself a favor and pitch for a financing to cover the base case budget plus 6 months of operations."

"If you are an entrepreneur pitching a novel biology story for seed financing to us at Atlas, we are going to deeply diligence the mechanism and / or technology. I often see pitches that are too high-level, which makes it challenging to dig into details and assess differentiation."

On the market as of mid-2025, and on the asset tilt:

"Let's level-set: This is one of the more difficult times to fundraise for a biotech company in the last decade"

"We are likely to continue to see capital skew towards the latter type of deal, with concentration in clinical-stage, asset-centric plays."

"Based on an analysis of recent Pitchbook data, I estimate >$55B in dry powder (largely for private deals) across >200 life sciences VCs in the U.S. and EU."

On the sheer attrition — useful for calibrating the group's expectations:

"If you are pitching a seed concept, maybe that total number is 50."

"It is not atypical to see some investments (regardless of whether they are Seed or Series C) take 9-12 months to close, and these days it may take even longer."

Source: lifescivc.com, July 2025

Atlas Venture — Aimee Raleigh, "Deconstructing the Diligence Process: An Approach to Vetting New Product Theses", LifeSciVC, April 2024. This is the single most informative document found on what actually counts as evidence at the seed/creation stage, and it is notable for what it omits (see §3b).

"Sometimes people are disappointed to learn there is no official checklist investors use during a diligence, but that's the beauty of working in the complex space of early-stage therapeutics!"

The stated evidence hierarchy begins with human genetics:

"In order to start building a case for or against a target, I like to start with genetics – first human and then mouse."

"While supportive genetics aren't absolutely required to move forward with a novel target, they certainly help to drive conviction in potential clinical relevance."

On what unifies investors across stages:

"What tends to be unifying is investors' desire to fund through key de-risking milestones in a given financing. Frequently, de-risking is perceived as 'proof of concept' (PoC) in the clinic for the lead asset, which is typically a Phase 1b or Phase 2a trial in a desired patient population with a registration (or directionally translational to registration) endpoint readout."

And the alternative inflection points, which matter for an AI company that cannot reach clinical PoC on seed money:

"These alternative inflection points include IND clearance, Phase 1 MAD data (which, especially for indications such as obesity where healthy volunteer data is well-precedented as a benchmark, can drive substantial value), and Phase 1 safety datasets if adverse events are a concern for the mechanism."

Her summary of what every diligence reduces to:

"every diligence usually involves some flavor of [therapeutic relevance], [tractability / pharmacology] (or clear path to proving or disproving the PK/PD/efficacy relationship), [a de-risking plan] that can be financed with venture dollars, and conviction that the [product thesis] merits the time and investment required to bring a new medicine to patients."

(Square brackets mark headings the page renders as styled fragments; the sentence structure and the words outside brackets are verbatim.)

Source: lifescivc.com, April 2024. Companion post on pharmacology: lifescivc.com, April 2024.

1d. Analyst accounts of the bar (not investors' own words, but concrete)

Jonathan Algoo and Richard Murphey, Bay Bridge Bio, "The top biotech seed investors, and how to raise from them" (undated page; data stated "as of Q4 2020"; footer © 2025). This is the most concrete published account of the variation in seed criteria. It is an analyst's account of what investors want, not an investor's own statement.

"Every investor will have a different set of criteria for when they can invest in a company. Some will want solid animal data, some will want a lead compound with composition of matter IP, some will want a good publication and line-of-sight to licensing key technology, some will invest in anything that comes out of certain accelerators, some will invest based on an impressive founding team and an idea."

"It is unlikely you'll be able to raise a seven-figure seed round with just a paper or a patent. You will need to execute on your idea for a year or more to prove to investors your idea is viable and your team is capable."

On how to size the round:

"you may determine that successful data in a gold standard animal model will be the best way to increase the value of your company, and you need $1M to reach that point, so your target raise is $1M."

On terms, explicitly dated:

"If you raise from Series A biotech investors, you will likely raise $30-50M+ in exchange for 30-50% of your company. If you are too early for Series A investors, you will raise from seed investors, and will probably raise a few million in exchange for 10-30%+ of your company. If you are too early for seed investors, you will need to raise from angels or an accelerator, and will likely raise six-figures in exchange for 10-20%+ of your company." … "Note: these numbers are for Silicon Valley-based investors as of Q4 2020."

On the techbio/biotech split, which matters for an AI company choosing a syndicate:

"One of the major differences between these categories of investor is that tech-bio investors are much more active and founder-friendly at the seed stage, so they are your best bet for raising initial capital."

2. The platform versus asset debate

2a. How the investors themselves frame the two models

Bruce Booth, Partner, Atlas Venture, "Biotech Risk Cycles: Assets And Platforms", 28 October 2024. This is the best single primary source on the debate and all quotations below are raw-verified.

Opening, as a statement of the prevailing market view (he then argues against it):

"Today's market likes products. Platforms aren't in vogue anymore. Investors, especially in the public markets, only want late stage de-risked assets. Pharma only seems to be buying these kinds of asset. VCs need to focus on clinical stage companies. Or so the conventional wisdom goes in the equity capital markets these days."

His definition of the two models:

"biotech business models have largely had two flavors for decades: asset-centric investments focused on specific product opportunities and platforms (discovery engines) designed to create new drugs based on novel modalities, technologies, or biological insights."

"The former is narrower in focus and typically more incremental, the latter broader in aperture often more transformational"

An important caution against the group over-reading the dichotomy:

"the reality on the ground in biotech companies is more of a fluid continuum than a polar dichotomy between these two models; unsurprisingly, this is a nuance lost in the simpler narrative of assets vs platforms."

Source: lifescivc.com, 28 October 2024

2b. How the fashion moved — dated statements and data

Booth's cycle account (28 October 2024). He identifies three "asset-in, platforms-out" periods in 25 years and ties them to the cost of capital:

"Over the past 25 years, we've witnessed three significant periods of time where this 'asset-in, platforms-out' psychology has been embraced, and they always occur during 'risk-off' periods when the equity capital markets tighten up."

"From 2009-2012, spec pharma and later stage asset plays were back in vogue, and platforms were really tough to gain market traction."

"The 2013-2021 period was an incredible secular bull market for biotech … 'Risk-on' was back, and platforms were cool again"

"Instead of starting 60-80 companies a quarter, the sector tried to start 3-4x that number."

On 2024 specifically:

"As the dust settled, the markets again cycled back to an 'assets-in, platforms-out' sentiment. This risk-off psychology sent funds predominantly to product stories, often more incremental in nature. As evidence of that, the vast majority of the IPOs this year are later stage assets, often in Phase 3, and frequently against validated targets rather than novel biology."

"The risk-off 'low innovation quotient' playbook is certainly back in favor in 2024."

And his own position, which is a contrarian one — worth flagging as such, since Atlas has platform exposure to defend:

"The resetting of the market in the past two years has been a healthy one for the long term … But there's a point where the pendulum between assets and platforms has swung too far, and we might be reaching it."

A directly relevant historical note for AI/computational platforms specifically:

"It took us two years to raise a small Series A for Nimbus (with two corporate VCs), which we co-founded in spring of 2009 as a new platform for computer-aided drug discovery with Schrodinger."

And on the reputational spillover from the bubble-era platform failures — the mechanism by which an AI startup today pays for others' claims:

"Many crazy science project 'platforms' were launched with hypelines and mega-rounds during the recent bubble. Some blew up nearly as quickly as they appeared (e.g., Tome, Saliogen) and many have had to retool/refocus and scale back their aspirations. Beyond burning lots of investor capital, these high profile challenges also sully the name of scientifically-sound and financially-prudent platform efforts – creating blow back that hurts the underwriting of risk subsequently."

The one published quantitative ratio. Uciane Scarlett (described by BioCentury as most recently a principal at MPM BioImpact, previously Oxford Science Enterprises and Atlas Venture), guest commentary, BioCentury, 13 January 2025. As fetched, she calls the "steady state" platform-to-asset ratio "the true north for VC funding" at "2:1 globally or 3:1 in the U.S.", cites roughly 1:2 (asset-tilted) in 2010–13 and a 4:1 platform peak in 2020–22, and writes:

"Over-indexing on platforms during 2020–22 saw early innovations driving limited exit opportunities."

"This trend is still in play and will likely continue to play out in 2025 and possibly early 2026."

"Platforms will continue to play a crucial role in rebuilding the innovation funnel for VCs."

Caveat recorded at the time of fetching: the piece labels the ratio inconsistently as global or U.S. across periods, so the numbers should be checked against the source charts before being quoted as data. The quotations above come from a page summary (the article is behind BioCentury's wall for raw access), so treat them as reported rather than raw-verified.

Source: biocentury.com, 13 January 2025

"From RTW's vantage point and reinforced by several VC conversations on the sidelines of JPM26, asset-focused companies remain more attractive than pure platform or technology plays in the current environment."

2c. What a platform must show, versus an asset

Bay Bridge Bio (Richard Murphey), "Biotech platforms are out, products are back in" (undated; internal evidence places it in 2022–23 — it analyses IPOs "from 2010-2022"). This is an analyst's work, not an investor statement, but it is the most quantitative public treatment found and it answers the "what must each show" question directly.

On the outcome data:

"Seven of the top 10 biggest underperformers were platform biotech companies. On the other hand, all of the biggest outperformers had derisked assets (approved products or products in pivotal studies)."

"SPACs represent 5% of total new public market entrances but account for 50% of the 10 worst-performing stocks. Similarly, tech-bio represents 5% of total biopharma public market entrances and represents 50% of the 10 worst-performing stocks by market cap."

The sentence most directly on point for this group's question — and note it concedes that seed is the one place a pure platform can still be funded:

"Platform risk is the last risk that biotech investors take over the course of a business cycle – and the first risk that they shed when the market turns. Today it is still possible to raise seed or Series A funding around a platform with no line-of-sight to development candidates, but downstream funding markets are more challenging for platforms."

On the historical rarity of pure-platform funding:

The criticism, as stated in investor-adjacent primary sources. The strongest version found is not framed as "you will become a CRO" but as "your partnerships will not be valued". Bay Bridge Bio, on CRISPR Therapeutics:

3. The AI-specific scepticism

3a. Investors discounting AI claims

Bruce Booth, Partner, Atlas Venture, "The Fruits of AI Drug Abundance: Indigestion In The Near Term", LifeSciVC, 30 October 2026. All quotations raw-verified. This is the most substantial recent statement by a seed-stage biotech investor on AI drug discovery.

His credential-then-caveat framing:

"I've invested actively in advances in computational drug discovery (now often called AI) for two decades, so I'm a believer. That said, many of the current hyperbolic claims are over-hyped, in my opinion, especially as they relate to upending the industry's R&D model in the near-term (or curing all disease)."

His prediction:

"AI will drive a wave of drug abundance and exacerbate the industry's aggregate R&D productivity challenges over the next 3-5 years, rather than improve those metrics."

On the moat question, directly relevant to whether an AI seed company has defensible IP:

"most 'proprietary' AI models quickly disseminate in various incarnations across the drug discovery industry. Closed and open models alike are and will be used to drive new molecular designs. Competitive moats are harder in an era of AI proliferation, for both foundational labs and the drug-designer application layer."

On the capital flowing in regardless:

"especially since 'AI native' startups are raising so much capital."

Source: lifescivc.com, 30 October 2026

Booth, a year earlier, in Atlas's annual state-of-the-sector presentation, as reported and quoted by David Shaywitz, Timmerman Report, 19 November 2024. Booth's words, quoted in the piece:

"AI and machine learning will have targeted impacts up and down the R&D process."

"Fundamentally, in the drug R&D space, given the complexity of human biology and the lack of massive datasets, we think that the impact of AI machine learning on R&D is going to be more likely around evolution than revolution. We're not going to see drug discovery go from a multiyear process to just weeks or days."

He also described the "incredible amount of buzz and hype" in the space as making it "difficult to figure out where the reality is", while maintaining "The reality is there."

Shaywitz's own characterisation of the Atlas position — his paraphrase, not Booth's words, and worth flagging as the single most load-bearing sentence for this group's question:

"If AI contributes to the development of a promising target or approach, they're all for it, but they're not looking to AI itself as the basis for a platform, nor do they see AI as the transformative unlock for biomedical discovery."

Shaywitz's framing of the geography of the disagreement:

"in the Bay Area, AI is what's captivating everyone … The attitude in Boston is, for the most part, rather more reserved."

"ML just isn't on the radar for most of New England's investment crowd, which sticks closely to traditional biotech."

Source: timmermanreport.com, 19 November 2024

3b. Is the AI required to be load-bearing, and how is it tested?

The strongest finding here is an absence, and it is a reliable one. Atlas Venture's published, step-by-step diligence framework for a new therapeutic thesis (April 2024) runs through: human and mouse genetics (OMIM, GWAS Catalog, DisGeNET, OpenTargets, Genebass, IMPC are the named resources), direction of pathology and choice of modality, PK/PD/efficacy relationship, biomarker availability, path to clinical proof of concept, and target product profile. It does not mention AI, machine learning, computation, or any model-quality criterion at any point. Whatever a seed-stage AI company is asked for by this firm, it is not a demonstration that its model works; it is a demonstration that the target and the molecule are credible by conventional means.

This is consistent with Booth's statement of the test he applies to AI companies' output, which is a counterfactual-by-inspection rather than a diligence protocol:

"Just look at the pipelines of most of the major 'AI Native' discovery shops who claim to be using transformative AI models for drug discovery: they are mostly populated with well-credentialled targets and lots of pre-existing chemotypes or protein sequences: known chemical scaffold-target pairs (e.g., MEK, HDAC, KRAS, TYK2, 5HT, etc…), validated antibodies (e.g., TSLP, IL13), precedented T-cell engagers (e.g., CD3-CD19), and common ADCs (e.g., HER2, TROP2), among others."

"I've yet to see repeatable, scalable examples of generative AI finding totally new scaffolds for novel drugs against conventionally undruggable targets: using AI to tackle grievous diseases with first-in-class novel drug matter."

"Fundamentally, if today's AI were excelling at novel medicine creation, we'd see tons of new programs addressing targets we'd barely heard of, with both known modalities and also uniquely-AI-discovered drug scaffolds – but this isn't really happening."

He names the exceptions as exceptions:

"And, for the record, there are some novel AI-driven programs (e,g., In Silico's TNIK inhibitor, …), but they are the exception rather than the norm."

And names two 2026 "AI native" IPOs by program, as his evidence:

"Two biotech IPOs this year by 'AI native' players, Generate and Iambic, are working on TSLP and HER2; these may very well have credible shots of 'best in class' but they certainly aren't 'novel' programs."

Source: lifescivc.com, 30 October 2026

An explicit, documented diligence procedure for testing whether AI was load-bearing — e.g. a required ablation, a retrospective hold-out, a "would you have found this anyway?" exercise — was not found in any investor's published writing. Searched for and not located.

3c. Label quality, assay noise, and the measurement floor

This is where the published material is richest, and it corroborates the group's prior finding.

Daphne Koller, CEO and Founder, insitro, "Drug Discovery Has No Magic Wands", 3 August 2026 (published on insitro's deepphenotype Substack; cross-posted by a16z, per the piece's own note). Koller is a founder, not an investor — but this is the most cited recent statement of the data problem, and the a16z cross-post makes it an investor-endorsed position. All quotations raw-verified.

The central claim:

"AI is undoubtedly extraordinary, but aimed at a biology we have only begun to measure and barely understand, it will mostly help us generate failures faster."

On where the bottleneck is:

"The real bottleneck in making a novel medicine is disease understanding: identifying a biological mechanism whose modification actually changes the course of disease in patients."

"We are doing a pretty good job at manufacturing keys, but they are often for the wrong locks. Even if AI lets us make better keys at an accelerating pace, that won't improve our ability to identify the right locks."

On drugging the undruggable, as direct as it gets:

"as of now, I don't know of a single example of an AI-derived insight that has led to 'drugging the undruggable.'"

On the label/scorecard problem specifically — this is the measurement-floor argument stated in full:

"agents thrive when there is a fast, accurate, and cheap scorecard to evaluate progress. If you give a sufficiently smart model an instant feedback loop, it will grind against that benchmark until it wins."

"Drug development is the exact opposite. The ultimate scorecard — whether a drug actually provides therapeutic benefit to a patient — cannot be captured well by computational models or high-throughput assays. The only true ground truth is a human clinical trial."

"You cannot solve the human translation problem by accelerating our ability to optimize the wrong objective function."

"if you accelerate a pipeline full of drugs aimed at the wrong mechanisms, all you get is faster failures"

On data scale, with her own numbers:

"The largest cell atlases assembled to date, now spanning hundreds of millions of cells, remain orders of magnitude too small to cover this space."

"There are currently 37 targets that have over 50 programs against each of them"

"the number of novel targets the industry advances each year fell from ~100 in 2015 to about 30 in 2024."

On why biology does not transfer the way protein folding does:

"The folding of a single protein is a self-contained process, highly conserved — closer to physics than to biology … Brain function and dysfunction involves dozens of distinct cellular identities; and these processes are exquisitely specialized to humans: rodents do not get Alzheimer's disease; non-human primates do not recapitulate ALS."

Marketing caveat: the essay ends "This harder but aspirational path is the one we have elected to take at insitro." It is a manifesto for insitro's strategy as well as an analysis.

Source: deepphenotype.substack.com, 3 August 2026

The peer-reviewed version of the same argument. Bender, Scannell, Shaywitz, Grisoni, Cortes-Ciriano, Seal, Greener and others, "Artificial intelligence in drug discovery — what it is, where we stand and the path forward", Nature Reviews Drug Discovery, published online 7 August 2026 (November 2026 issue). From the abstract, raw-verified:

"Although a wide variety of AI methods have been developed, applied and benchmarked, evidence of their clinically relevant impact is, so far, disappointingly limited."

The abstract's named causes are directly about labels and problem specification:

"including an insufficient focus on clinical translation during model development, difficulties with applying AI algorithms on conditional life science data, and insufficient problem definitions and the resulting underspecification of computational models for real-world use cases."

And its recommendation, which is precisely the benchmark critique:

"benchmarking studies of AI tools in drug discovery need to move on from model validation and instead focus on their ability to improve decision making."

3d. The reframing that actually answers the question: model validation versus process validation

David Shaywitz, co-author of the Bender et al. Perspective, "AI in Drug R&D: At Last, the End of the Beginning", Timmerman Report, 14 August 2026. Raw-verified. This is the clearest published statement of the test the group is asking about — not "is the model good?" but "did the model change a decision?"

"A model operates inside an actual project and is supposed to inform an actual decision. Does it change which experiment gets run, which compound advances, or which weak program gets killed? Without those connections to project context and downstream decision-making, better model performance need not translate into better drug discovery."

"Two models can look nearly identical on a standard overall performance measure and behave completely differently in use: one might be much better at surfacing the few compounds worth advancing, while the other might be better at rejecting compounds with liabilities. There is no context-free definition of a good model; the relevant question is what the model is good for."

"A gorgeous model may represent a succès d'estime and a prestige publication. What matters is whether it improves consequential R&D decisions."

On why AI work clusters where the labels are, which is the group's measurement-floor point stated as an incentive problem:

"Yet much AI work remains concentrated considerably earlier, particularly around hit and ligand discovery. The reason is understandable: that is where labeled data are plentiful and the modeling problems are more tractable. But it risks becoming the familiar search for the keys under the streetlight, focusing on what we can readily model rather than what most needs improving."

On the historical bar, attributing to co-author Jack Scannell:

"As Scannell points out, if AI actually bends that trajectory, it will have accomplished something that other extraordinarily important innovations did not. That sets a considerably higher bar for 'transformation' than demonstrating impressive technical performance."

Source: timmermanreport.com, 14 August 2026

The one source found that states the counterfactual diligence question in so many words is a venture studio's content-marketing page with no named author — HealthSeed AG (Zürich), "AI Drug Discovery: What Investors Should Ask", 22 August 2026. "Ask for the decision points where a model changed the answer, and for what the team would have done without it."

3f. Whether a proprietary wet-lab data factory is a moat — the argument against

This directly contradicts the data-moat thesis that LongeVC states in §3e, and it matters for any AI-bio company whose pitch is "we generate our own data". Shaywitz, 14 August 2026, attributing the economics to Jack Scannell. Raw-verified:

"a company that invents a proprietary molecule can generally capture much of the value it creates. A better translational model, assay or validated biomarker also may benefit competitors. Developing that validated proxy can therefore be enormously valuable to patients and the field while harder for the organization that paid to create it to monetize. This suggests a potentially important role for disease foundations, public funders and carefully designed precompetitive consortia."

And the condition under which a data factory is worth building:

"That often means deliberately generating fit-for-purpose data rather than simply applying AI to whatever happens to be available. The critical step is establishing that an experimental proxy really predicts the in vivo or clinical outcome we care about; only then does it make sense to generate the data and models at scale."

On why volume is the wrong metric — the measurement-floor argument in its sharpest published form:

"This is why saying that biomedicine has enormous amounts of data can mislead. The problem is not simply quantity or technical quality. What matters … [is] whether the thing we can measure actually tells us something useful about the outcome we care about. Generating exquisitely reproducible data around a weak proxy mostly allows us to characterize the wrong thing with greater precision."

"A proxy is not a shortcut simply because it can be measured sooner. The work lies in establishing that the earlier signal actually predicts the later outcome we care about."

"Biological data are also highly conditional. … Sometimes even ostensibly the same experiment, using the same cell type and concentration, can give a materially different answer in another laboratory because variables that look incidental turn out to matter. This is also why simply pooling datasets can be deceptive."

On the epistemic problem:

"we often don't understand nearly well enough the relationship between the proxy we can readily measure and the clinical outcome we actually care about."

Source: timmermanreport.com, 14 August 2026

6. Non-dilutive and alternative paths

The one-line finding: every non-dilutive funder located describes its own exit as "attract investors and partners", not as a substitute for them. NIH says grantees "leverage funding to attract investors and partners"; Harvard's Blavatnik accelerator aims to reach the point "where an industry partnership can commence"; Breakout Labs funds the "transition from early start-up to venture-backed company". No programme officer or accelerator partner found claims the grant path reaches a seed bar on its own.

6a. NIH SBIR/STTR — the numbers and the framing

Current guideline award sizes, from two official sources that agree. SBA/SBIR.gov: "As of October 16, 2026, agencies may issue a Phase I award (including modifications) up to $335,937" and "a Phase II award (including modifications) up to $2,239,578 without seeking SBA approval." SBIR "provides equity free funding through federal agencies to American small businesses." (sbir.gov/about) NIH's SEED office gives the same figures with timelines: "A Phase I budget is $335,937 for a project timeline between 6 months to 2 years"; "A Phase II budget is $2,239,578 for a project timeline between 1 to 3 years." (seed.nih.gov)

Trajectory: NIH SEED's 2023 webinar deck gave "Phase I: $275,766/1-2 years" and "Phase II: $1,838,436/1-3 years", i.e. the guideline has risen roughly 22% in three years. (deck) Set-aside: NIH SEED currently states "These programs set aside $1.4 billion in non-dilutive funds every year" (link); the 2023 deck said $1.2 billion, split $1.1 billion SBIR / $150 million STTR.

What NIH programme staff say it is for. Stephanie Fertig, Director, NIH Small Business Program, in the SEED webinar "Navigating America's Seed Fund at NIH", August 2023 (quotations extracted from the transcript PDF by the parallel effort):

"This is one of the largest sources of early-stage capital for life sciences in the United States, and really, these provide small businesses the ability to take their innovations and derisk their technologies and allow awardees to leverage funding to attract investors and partners."

"One of the unique features of the NIH program compared to other agencies is that we tend not to be the final purchasers of our technology. So really, again, we're here to provide non-dilutive capital. It's not a loan. You don't have to pay it back."

Her explicit grants-versus-equity framing, presented as myth-busting — note NIH is advocating for its own programme here, so this is self-promotional:

"myth number one, and this is a myth I hear a lot, that it's just easier and better for a company to get investors and avoid all the time and work for an NIH grant." … "And I really want to bust that myth because many of our companies have developed their product with non-dilutive funding that is very attractive to investors and strategic partners."

On the gap SBIR cannot cross on its own:

"we do recognize that there might be a gap between the phase II and the commercial market, partner, or investor. And that's where we have some additional programs, an additional phase II, a competing renewal or the Commercialization Readiness Pilot, or CRP."

"if you take nothing else from this webinar, please contact a program officer well in advance of applying."

NCI SBIR, in its own words: "No repayment is required. It doesn't impact stock or shares in any way (i.e., non-dilutive)." (link)

VC-ownership eligibility, as NIH states it now. From SEED's eligibility page (fetched 11 November 2026): the standard route is to "Be a concern which is more than 50% directly owned and controlled by one or more individuals"; SBIR only additionally permits a concern "more than 50% owned by multiple venture capital operating companies, hedge funds" and private equity firms, provided "No single venture capital operating company, hedge fund, or private equity firm may own more than 50% of the concern". For STTR, companies "more than 50% owned by multiple venture capital operating companies … are NOT eligible to apply". Eligibility "is determined at the time of award. We do not determine eligibility prior to submission at the application" (Fertig). (link)

Any 2025–2026 change to NIH SBIR eligibility for VC-backed companies: not confirmed. The live rule is unchanged in substance from the SBA rule implementing the 2011–12 reauthorisation. Third-party claims encountered about a 2026 reauthorisation, a bill number, a $30M "Phase IIB Strategic Breakthrough Award" with a 100% match, and a five-month award freeze could not be verified against any primary source and should not be used.

6b. Do VCs treat SBIR as validating, or as a negative signal?

Not found, in either direction. No current on-the-record investor statement was located. Two indirect data points:

6c. ARPA-H

ARPA-H's SBIR/STTR awards are materially larger than NIH's: "Awards are generally issued in the form of contracts of up to $600K for Phase 1 and $3.5M for Phase 2 awards", with eligibility requiring "majority ownership by U.S. Citizens or another U.S. based small business" (link). At the time of fetching, no ARPA-H SBIR/STTR solicitations were open — it is an episodic rather than continuous option.

On mechanism, from ARPA-H's submission FAQ: the agency "primarily use[s] Other Transactions for our research and development (R&D) efforts"; "funds are typically disbursed after the performer successfully completes milestones"; "IP rights will be part of award negotiations between the proposer and ARPA-H." (link)

On the programme-manager model: "ARPA-H programs require building multiple performer teams; not relying on a single lab, company, or approach"; PMs "don't have a lab (ARPA-H has no labs)"; they "succeed by defining clear milestones and decision points, so progress is measurable and guided by evidence"; and the PM "will advise, adjust, re-scope, extend, or cancel projects based on performance throughout the contract." (link)

Named official on the record: Alicia Jackson, Ph.D., ARPA-H Director, 22 April 2026, announcing SBIR/STTR contracts to 15 small businesses totalling about $28M — "By mobilizing American small businesses, we're maintaining momentum at the leading edge of biomedical innovation" (link).

Whether ARPA-H substitutes for early equity: not found. No ARPA-H page or official statement located addresses equity, dilution, or substituting for private investment; the FAQs are silent. The defensible characterisation is that ARPA-H funds milestone-gated Other Transactions to multi-performer teams, takes no equity position described anywhere publicly, and negotiates IP per award — but no official is on record calling it an alternative to seed equity.

6g. The arithmetic, for the synthesis

NIH SBIR Phase I ($335,937) + Phase II ($2,239,578) = about $2.58M of equity-free capital over a 2–5 year clock. ARPA-H SBIR, when open: $600K + $3.5M = about $4.1M on milestone-gated contracts. Against those, a SOSV pre-seed cheque is up to $550K for more than 10% of the company, Petri's is $250,000, and Activate's is "More than $300K over two years … All for zero equity". The honest comparison is that non-dilutive sources can exceed accelerator equity cheques by five to eight times — but on a cycle time that no seed-stage company can plan hiring around, and with every funder describing its own role as getting the company to investors rather than past them.

7. Where investors' stated positions look inconsistent with the funding record

Flagged for the other fronts to test against their factual record. These are hypotheses about inconsistency, not findings of bad faith.

  3. Atlas's published position that AI is "evolution not revolution" and that they are "not looking to AI itself as the basis for a platform" (2024) versus the firm's own history of founding a computational drug discovery platform (Nimbus, 2009, with Schrödinger) and Booth's "I've invested actively in advances in computational drug discovery … for two decades" (2026). This is not a contradiction so much as a distinction the group should state precisely: computation as a capability inside a target-led company is funded; computation as the company's reason to exist is not. Test: classify Atlas's 2023–26 new company formations by whether the investment thesis is a target/mechanism or a computational method.

  4. "Platforms aren't in vogue anymore" (Booth, Oct 2024) and "capital [skews] towards … clinical-stage, asset-centric plays" (Raleigh, Jul 2025) versus the fact that AI-native platform companies kept raising very large rounds through the same period — Booth himself notes "'AI native' startups are raising so much capital" (Oct 2026). The stated preference and the observed flow of capital point in opposite directions for this one segment. Test: total dollars into AI-native discovery platforms by year 2023–26 against total into asset-centric newcos, and whether the AI platform money came from biotech funds or from generalist/tech funds.

  5. The platform:asset ratio claims conflict in the public record. Scarlett's BioCentury commentary (Jan 2025) says the sector is reverting from a 4:1 platform peak toward a 2:1/3:1 steady state — i.e. platforms still take the majority. Bay Bridge Bio says pure platforms have received significant funding in only "a handful of years" in 25. These can be reconciled only by a definition of "platform" that differs between the two. Test: pin down each source's definition before using either number.

  6. Investor essays describe a genetics-first, pharmacology-anchored evidence standard that most AI seed companies cannot meet, yet those companies are funded. Atlas's diligence framework (Apr 2024) asks for human genetics, PK/PD and a financeable path to clinical PoC. An AI-first seed company typically has none of these. Test: for AI drug discovery seed rounds 2023–26, who led — biotech specialists who publish this standard, or generalist/tech investors who do not?

  7. "It is unlikely you'll be able to raise a seven-figure seed round with just a paper or a patent" (Bay Bridge Bio, Q4 2020 data) versus the 2021–2024 cohort of model-first companies that appear to have raised on a paper and a preprint. Test: the group's own case studies should be able to settle this directly; if a meaningful number raised seven-figure seeds on publication alone, the stated bar was simply not the operative one for AI.

  8. "The due diligence bar has risen significantly" (LongeVC, May 2026) versus the absence of any published AI-specific diligence standard. Firms assert a higher bar for AI companies while publishing no criterion by which an AI claim could fail. The only stated counterfactual test found is on an unsigned venture-studio marketing page. Test: ask the group's case-study companies what their seed investors actually asked them to demonstrate about the model.

  9. The data-moat claim and the data-economics argument cannot both be right. LongeVC's requirement ("datasets that competitors can't easily replicate") is the standard AI-bio pitch; Scannell's argument via Shaywitz is that a validated assay or biomarker is precisely the kind of asset whose value leaks to competitors. Test: for AI-bio companies that built proprietary data-generation capacity 2021–26, did any capture value from the data itself — licensing, exclusivity premiums in partnerships — as opposed to from the molecules it produced?

  10. "AI Native" success-rate claims versus pipeline composition. A PitchBook report reportedly claimed near-double early clinical success rates for AI-native biotechs; Booth's response is that those pipelines run on precedented targets and scaffolds, which would also predict higher early success for reasons having nothing to do with AI. Test: stratify AI-native clinical success rates by target novelty. If the advantage disappears once precedented targets are controlled for, the statistic measures target choice, not AI.

  11. Flagship and Third Rock describe doing at the seed stage what founders are told to arrive with. Third Rock's "extended time we take at the seed stage, validating mechanisms and maturing a lead program well before a company launches" is the same work an outside founder must self-fund to clear the bar. Test: compare the capital consumed before first external round for venture-created versus founder-created AI-bio companies. If it is comparable, the difference is who bears the risk, not how much evidence is required.

  12. Firms' claims to be research-driven and thesis-led are hard to audit because their own writing is unreadable. Dimension, Compound, Lux, Khosla and Founders Fund all present as thesis-publishing firms but serve their content in ways that resist retrieval. Worth noting in the synthesis that for several of the most active AI-bio investors, there is no accessible published statement of criteria at all — which should make the group sceptical of any secondhand characterisation of what they require.
