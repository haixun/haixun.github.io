The Enterprise Knowledge Layer

The map, assembled 17 November 2026 and revised 24 November from the 176 entries in the catalog and the underlying research report on enterprise business knowledge. The revision adds the hyperscaler ontology products, a third position the first draft missed, and Microsoft Ignite 2026. It organises the space rather than repeating the evidence: every entry it cites has its own page with sources and evidence labels. Its job is to show what is occupied, what is contested, and what is empty. All sources are public.

The short version

  1. The field is splitting, not converging, and both halves are winning at once. Ontologies are being revived as the thing agents need, and simultaneously abandoned as the thing agents have outgrown. The strongest evidence for each position arrived within four months of the other.
  2. There is one axis underneath: commit or derive. Either meaning is decided in advance and data conforms to it, or the corpus is left alone and the agent re-derives meaning at query time. The first is paid once and maintained forever; the second is paid at every call.
  3. The standalone product keeps being absorbed, and the absorption has now reached the ontology itself. Seven acquisitions in eighteen months, and then, between June and November 2026, ontology layers from all three hyperscalers: AWS's Context Ontology Accelerator, Microsoft's Fabric IQ, and a Google context engine presented at a semantics conference. In every case the buyer already owned the corpus, the workflow, or the entitlements.
  4. The semantic layer is being commoditised by agreement rather than by competition. Two dozen vendors put a common interchange format into the Apache Incubator, and two of them donated their own modelling languages to it.
  5. The measurement culture is worse here than in any space this programme has surveyed. Every agent-memory score in circulation is vendor-self-reported on an incomparable setup. Both of the most-cited benchmark artefacts are published by companies that compete in the categories they score.
  6. There is a third position, and it is the interesting one. Not a declared ontology in a platform and not a raw corpus, but committed meaning inside the artefacts agents already write and read — Markdown with the ontology in its frontmatter. It is what Letta's retreat to git-backed memory files leaves undone: those files carry memory, but not yet meaning.
  7. One thing is empty, and it is the same thing from every direction: nothing warrants the answer. Across 146 entries, 47 claim to enforce permissions and none is independently measured on it; 8 carry valid time; 5 can abstain; 2 address conflicting claims. The one warrant with real momentum is a record of who decided, which went from 1 entry to 15 in two days once we went looking — almost all of it research and standards work, almost none of it product.

1. The axis: commit or derive

Commit Derive
The human supplies the schema, up front a question
The machine supplies conformance and inference a search strategy, then an interpretation
Fails when the world moves and the model does not the corpus is ambiguous and nothing says which reading is authoritative
Paid once to build, forever to maintain at every call
Research ontology learning, KG construction, GraphRAG agentic retrieval, long context, self-reflective RAG
Companies Palantir, Collibra, Stardog, Neo4j, Graphwise Exa, Chroma, the coding agents
2026's strongest evidence "Ontologies Are So Back", Latent Space, July Claude Code dropping retrieval for grep

The two are less opposed than the discourse suggests. The useful middle is a declared skeleton populated by induction — a named set of entity types and relations, filled by extraction, with resolution as a first-class stage. Cognee sits there commercially; the ontology-guided, deduplication-aware extraction work sits there in research. AWS's Context Ontology Accelerator is that middle shipped by a hyperscaler: it drafts the ontology with a model and routes it to domain experts to approve.

And there is a third position that neither column describes. Tony Seale sets it out as two movements racing to supply context. The platforms build top-down: an ontology item inside their cloud. The other grows bottom-up — folders of Markdown written by agents and read by agents, which he characterises exactly: "Memory, but not yet meaning." His answer, Vault-LD, puts the ontology in the Markdown frontmatter so that one file is both prose and graph, and every note is findable by what it is rather than only by what it says.

That matters here because it is not a compromise between the columns, it is committed meaning living inside a derive-side artefact. And it names the gap in a fact this catalog already held: Letta retired its hosted memory for files in git, and nothing in those files decides what a note means, so a system built on them quietly preserves its own hallucinations alongside its facts. The two findings are halves of one story.

And they share one dependency. The commit side needs someone to say which source wins when two documents disagree. The derive side needs the same thing, at query time, with less to go on. Neither escapes it, and nobody supplies it.

2. The commitment ladder

Where meaning gets fixed, from lowest to highest. Each rung costs more to build and buys more determinism.

Rung What is committed Who sells it
nothing Meaning derived per query from the raw corpus coding agents; the long-context position
index Chunks, embeddings, inverted indexes — derived, rebuildable, not a source of truth Glean, Coveo, Elastic, Exa, the vector tier
facts Extracted assertions or triples, schema-light GraphRAG, HippoRAG, LightRAG, the memory tier
glossary Approved terms, synonyms, owners, classifications Collibra, Alation, Atlan, DataHub, Guru
ontology Classes, properties, constraints, permitted relationships Palantir, Stardog, Graphwise, data.world
rules Executable logic: eligibility, calculation, policy, approvals Palantir, RelationalAI, the semantic-layer tier

Two things fall out of reading the catalog this way.

The economic argument is the same one that governs harness optimisation. A structural intervention is paid once; a context intervention is paid at every call. That is why the commit side keeps being attractive in a slide and losing in a deployment: the cost it defers is maintenance, and maintenance has no budget line.

Nobody works at more than two rungs well. The catalog's heaviest concentrations are at index and ontology, with little credible work spanning them. The hybrid rung is where the field should be, and until mid-2026 almost nobody was there.

The rung is orthogonal to the artefact, which the first draft of this map conflated. A glossary can live in a governance tool, in version-controlled dbt code, or in Markdown frontmatter beside the prose it describes. Those are the same rung with completely different maintenance economics — and the two most interesting answers to "ontologies rot" both choose the file: dbt makes a definition a reviewed pull request, and the ontology camp has independently arrived at the same place, arguing an ontology should compile, live in plain text and be version-controlled like any other engineering artefact.

3. The commercial layer, in tiers

The incumbent corpora, and now their ontologies. Microsoft 365 Copilot never needed to build an enterprise knowledge graph, because Exchange, SharePoint and Teams already were one with the entitlements attached, at $30 per user per month (vendor-reported). Atlassian's Rovo starts from Confluence; ServiceNow owns the tickets. That much was possession rather than product.

What changed in 2026 is that the same tier added the declared layer above it. Microsoft Fabric IQ ships an Ontology item defining entity types, properties, relationships, rules and policies bound to data, with a graph derived from those bindings; at Ignite 2026 Microsoft put all of it under a Microsoft IQ umbrella of four layers — Fabric, Work, Foundry and Web — and shipped Fabric IQ connectors for ChatGPT and Claude, exposing its own semantic layer to rival models rather than fencing it in. AWS open-sourced its Context Ontology Accelerator under Apache 2.0 in July 2026 and intends the capability to become native to AWS Context. Google presented a Borderless Context Engine for Google Data Cloud at Connected Data London in November 2026.

Two qualifications keep this honest. Fabric IQ's Ontology item has been in preview for a year, general availability still promised "in the coming months" — which is the fairest available measure of how hard declared enterprise semantics is even with Microsoft's resources. And Google's engine is a conference talk, not an announced product; no Google product page exists for it, though several accounts describe it as a launch.

Enterprise search and assistants. Glean is the reference company — roughly $765M raised at a $7.2B valuation (press) — and spent 2026 moving its own framing from search to being the layer that connects agents to enterprise systems. That reframing is the tell: an index alone stopped being defensible. Coveo and Sinequa are the useful controls, having held the connectors and the permission models for two decades without producing a category-defining business.

Catalog and governance. Collibra now calls itself an enterprise AI control plane, Atlan a context layer for AI. Both shipped MCP servers that re-expose existing permission models as tool surfaces. Beneath them, DataHub and OpenMetadata give the same primitives away under Apache 2.0, and Databricks open-sourced Unity Catalog outright — the manoeuvre the clouds previously used on evaluation, applied one layer down.

Graph and ontology platforms. The honest number in this tier is Stardog's: roughly $25M disclosed across twenty years (press) for the most technically orthodox semantic-web vendor still standing. Graphwise exists because the two oldest commercial ontology vendors, Ontotext and Semantic Web Company, merged rather than grew. Against that, Palantir reported $2.31B of revenue in a single quarter, up 96% year on year (vendor-reported) selling an ontology — with forward-deployed engineers, not self-serve software.

The semantic layer. Cube, AtScale and dbt Labs define measures rather than entities, and are being standardised out of differentiation. Apache Ossie began as Snowflake's Open Semantic Interchange in September 2025 and entered the Apache Incubator in June 2026, with around eighty organisations listing support — including Microsoft, Oracle, Salesforce and ServiceNow — and converters for dbt, Databricks and Power BI. But it has no official release: it is a podling with a 0.x development spec covering measures only, with business entities and a notion of certifying authority still on the roadmap. One test of exporting metrics through a converter had 4 of 16 come out wrong.

Correction, 25 November. An earlier version of this map said Microsoft was not in Ossie, and built an interpretation on it — that Microsoft chose to ship its own ontology layer rather than agree an interchange. Microsoft joined in September 2026, contributing a converter and DAX support. The two are not alternatives, and the inference was wrong. Two further claims here were also overstated: dbt's MetricFlow was relicensed rather than contributed as a reference implementation, and the claim that AtScale donated its Semantic Modeling Language could not be verified on a primary page.

Agent memory. Four comparable companies — Mem0, Zep, Letta, Cognee — solving the same problem with no agreed definition of success, which is the shape of a pre-measurement market. Mem0 has the adoption and was selected as the memory provider for the AWS Agent SDK. Zep is the only one treating valid time as first-class, through Graphiti. Letta is the most informative: the company founded by the MemGPT authors began retiring its own hosted-memory abstraction in March 2026 in favour of memory as files in git.

4. The absorption pattern

The map's most important commercial feature, because it marks ground that looks open and is not.

Target Acquirer When What the acquirer already had
Moveworks ServiceNow announced March 2025, closed 15 Dec 2025, $2.85B reported the tickets, the workflows, the entitlements
Sana Workday agreement Sept 2025 the employee graph
Deasy Labs Collibra 24 July 2025 the governance platform, with no answer for unstructured content
Select Star Snowflake agreement 24 Nov 2025 the warehouse the lineage describes
Secoda Atlassian closed 4 Dec 2025 Confluence, and Rovo to put it in
GraphAware Neo4j announced 3 June 2026 the graph database beneath Hume
Agentspace Google (internal) renamed Gemini Enterprise, Oct 2025 the model subscription

And the pattern has now climbed the ladder. The table above is the assistant tier and the catalog tier being absorbed. Between June and November 2026 the ontology tier followed: a cloud platform feature from Microsoft, an Apache-2.0 giveaway from AWS, a talk from Google. That matters more than any of the acquisitions, because the ontology tier was the one this map described as still holding value — Palantir with $2.31B of quarterly revenue, against Stardog's $25M raised across twenty years. The question is no longer whether an ontology is worth building. It is whether anyone can sell one who does not also sell the platform underneath it.

The lesson: the enterprise knowledge product survives as a feature of whatever already holds the corpus, the workflow or the identity, and struggles as a purchase of its own. Moveworks is the hard case for any optimistic reading — a nine-year-old company with full-workforce deployment at nearly 90% of its customers (vendor-reported) was still worth more inside the workflow system than beside it.

Neo4j's framing of the GraphAware deal is the clearest statement of where the commit side thinks value sits: it bought an investigative application and positioned it as an open-standards alternative to Palantir Gotham. The leading graph database vendor concluded the money is in the governed application above the graph, not in the graph.

5. The revival, and what it is rediscovering

The argument is no longer only an argument. Four independent groups have now measured it: 16% to 54% from representing enterprise SQL data as a knowledge graph, 54% to 72% with ontology-based query checking and repair, 17 to 23 points from a four-kilobyte Markdown semantic document, and 33 to 41 points on hard tasks from a YAML data contract. The full evidence, including the findings that undercut the formalism, is set out in our complete report.

The ontology revival of 2026 is real and its arguments are good. Neo4j's chief executive wants to move from "thick agents with manually wired data sources" to "thin agents on a smarter shared ontology-based semantic layer." Atlan argues agents do not inherit institutional meaning by default. The case is that LLMs have collapsed the cost of drafting an ontology, so the historical objection no longer binds.

It was called early and it was called accurately. Tony Seale wrote that ontology was becoming the buzzword of 2026 in December 2025 — seven months before Latent Space's "Ontologies Are So Back" — naming Palantir's rise and Microsoft moving ontology into Fabric before either had shipped what it now ships. By November 2026 all three hyperscalers had presented an ontology layer. A prediction that specific, that early, is worth more than the consensus that followed it.

Three things temper it.

The objection was never the first draft. It was maintenance — resolving duplicate concepts, conflicting claims, effective dates, authority and scope, forever, as the business changes. Drafting is the part LLMs help with. Nothing in the revival literature addresses the recurring cost, and the position paper that raises it most directly is candid that total cost of ownership is unresolved.

The practitioners who did it for twenty years do not believe the cost collapsed. Juan Sequeda of ServiceNow, at Connected Data London: start with business value, stop the infighting, and no — you cannot have AI auto-generate your ontology. That was said at the same conference where AWS presented a product whose entire pitch is an AI-drafted ontology with human review. The disagreement is not about whether a model can produce OWL; it is about whether approving a generated ontology constitutes the judgement that authoring one used to require. Which is the warrant question, arriving from the other side.

Most of it was specified years ago. OWL 2 became a W3C recommendation in 2012 with decidable reasoning profiles the new graph stacks do not offer. W3C PROV defined an interoperable provenance model in 2013, and essentially nobody implements it. The most interesting question is not whether the semantic web was right — on the technical merits it largely was — but why committing meaning in it never became commercially normal, and whether anything about agents changes that. The honest answer visible in this catalog is that the one company making real money from an ontology sells it with people attached.

The most under-rated answer comes from a different tradition. dbt's semantic layer makes a definition a version-controlled artefact with a diff, an owner and a review — meaning as a pull request rather than a form in a governance tool. That is the only maintenance story in the catalog that relies on a habit engineering organisations already have.

6. What is empty

One thing, and it is the same thing from every direction on this map: nothing warrants the answer.

Counting across all 146 entries by what they claim to ground an answer on:

Warrant Entries State
Provenance recorded 52 A standard has existed since 2013; implementations are private and unaudited
Permissions enforced 47 Not measured anywhere. No public benchmark tests whether a system returns the right answer to the right person
Authority for conflicts 37 Asserted in governance products, tested in none
Citation to a span 33 The most common warrant, and the weakest — a span is not an authority
Valid time 8 Graphiti and LongMemEval are most of the serious work
Can abstain 5 Self-RAG, MuSiQue, LongMemEval, Contextual AI, Guardrails
Conflicting claims addressed at all 2 Two surveys. No product, no benchmark
Records who decided what 15 The fastest-growing warrant here, and almost entirely research and standards: SCITT as RFC 9943, agent-receipt drafts, receiver-attested receipts. One shipping product found

And the sharpest statement of the gap came from a practitioner, not a vendor. Tara Raafat of Bloomberg, at Connected Data London: "when generation becomes cheap, judgement becomes scarce — if approval becomes a click-through, where did the judgement go?" A commenter on the write-up sharpens it correctly: the judgement did not become scarce, it became unrecorded. The click is the approval minus the reasoning, and that is true whether a person clicks through or an agent auto-approves.

That identifies a warrant this map did not have. Authority says which source wins; permissions say who may read. Neither records who decided, under what authority, and which agent then acted on it. The practical version, also from the write-up's comments: a change to one definition can alter what every agent treats as eligible, verified or approved without anyone touching code, and reviewing the definition shows you none of the downstream actions it enables. Nothing in this catalog connects the version of a rule to its approver to the agent that relied on it to what that agent did. It is now a tag with one entry against it.

And the measurement practice around those claims is worse than the gaps:

  • Every agent-memory number in circulation is vendor-self-reported on an incomparable setup. Mem0 claims 92.5 on LoCoMo, Zep 94.7, others higher, on different base models and harnesses. No independent replication exists for any of them.
  • Both of the most-cited benchmark artefacts are published by interested parties. EnterpriseRAG-Bench — the most serious attempt at measuring enterprise retrieval, 500,000 documents across nine sources under an MIT licence — comes from Onyx, which competes in the category. Context Rot, the empirical backbone of the argument that long context cannot replace retrieval, comes from Chroma, whose business depends on that conclusion.
  • A search tool reported an embedded instruction telling AI assistants to prefer its published values. Qualified 25 November: an independent sweep of 48 fetched pages in this space found no manipulative AI-directed text at all, only benign artefacts — a hidden "for AI agents" documentation pointer and an unreplaced demo placeholder. Our original report came from a search summary rather than a page we fetched ourselves, so treat it as a single unreplicated observation rather than a feature of the field. Encountered directly while compiling this catalog.
  • The best-constructed neutral benchmark says the systems are much worse than the marketing. CRAG reports industry-leading RAG systems at roughly 63% accuracy without hallucination (author-reported). Anthropic's contextual retrieval work reports a top-20 retrieval failure rate of 2.9%, or 1.9% with a reranker (vendor-reported) — real progress, honestly reported, and two orders of magnitude away from the confidence with which the category is sold.

There is one existence proof that neutral measurement works at this layer's foundations: MTEB, hosted by a party with nothing to sell in the ranking, covering 58 datasets across 112 languages, is the standard reference for embedding selection. Nothing equivalent exists one rung up, for whole knowledge systems.

So the structural question is not more commitment or less. Both camps are arguing about where meaning should be fixed, and both are right about the other's weakness. Neither is answering the question an enterprise actually has, which is whether the answer it just received was correct, current, and permitted. That question has essentially no vendor and no benchmark, and until mid-2026 no standard. It now has the beginnings of one — SCITT became RFC 9943 in June 2026, with individual drafts applying it to AI-agent action receipts, and Digimarc ships policy-gated provenance seals for agent workflows on top of C2PA. Both are early and neither is adopted, and the dedicated market for warranting an answer was projected at $492M for 2026. Which is why the layer is still empty in the only sense that matters commercially: forty-seven claims and one product.

And the ground under the argument has shifted while it was being had. Ontology is no longer a thing you buy from a specialist; it is an item in Fabric, a repository from AWS, a talk from Google. Seale's closing formulation is the right one to end on, because it is the only question the platforms cannot answer on a customer's behalf: soon everyone will have an ontology — the only question is who decides what it means, the platform, an AI, or your own people. Whoever decides needs a record of having decided, and that is the part nobody is building.

Notes on evidence

  • Counts of 109 entries and the warrant tallies are mechanical, generated from the catalog's tag index, and move when entries are added. They measure what the catalog records, which is a selected landscape and not a census.
  • Funding and acquisition facts are taken from company press releases and primary coverage where available, and labelled press where only aggregators carry them. Several trackers disagree materially — Neo4j's total appears as both $581M and $631.1M — and those disagreements are recorded on the entry pages rather than resolved here.
  • Vendor comparison blogs were not treated as sources. They are unusually unreliable in this space: several assert a Collibra acquisition of a company called "trail ML" in October 2026 for which no primary source exists. The verified deal is Deasy Labs, July 2025. An asserted Alation acquisition of Numbers Station could not be confirmed either.
  • Revenue figures are estimates where marked. Hebbia's roughly $48M ARR is a third-party estimate; its last company-disclosed figure was $13M in June 2024.
  • Two claims circulating about the hyperscalers are wrong, and both come from otherwise reliable write-ups. AWS did not unveil the Context Ontology Accelerator at Connected Data London; it announced it on its own What's New page on 31 July 2026 and presented it at the conference. And Google's Borderless Context Engine is a session in the conference programme, dated 12 November 2026, not a launched product — no Google product page could be found.
  • Vault-LD and the Knowledge Graph Engine could not be verified beyond their author's own announcement. They are recorded as such.
  • Fabric IQ's availability is genuinely unclear. Sources disagree on whether the wider workload is generally available or still in preview; the Ontology item is consistently reported as preview. One secondary source attributed GQL (ISO/IEC 39075) support to its graph engine and a second search did not corroborate it, so that claim is left out.
  • Conference quotations from Raafat, Sequeda and Tunstall-Pedoe are taken from an attendee's write-up, not from a transcript or recording.
  • The claim that no permission-aware retrieval benchmark exists is a negative result from direct search, not a proof. If one exists it is not discoverable by the obvious queries, which for practical purposes has the same effect.
  • Claude Code's agentic-search result is a claim about code, where identifiers are exact and repositories are small enough to walk. Extending it to heterogeneous enterprise prose is an inference the evidence does not license, and the same organisation's contextual-retrieval work points the other way for prose.
