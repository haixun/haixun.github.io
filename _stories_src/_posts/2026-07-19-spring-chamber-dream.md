When our daughter was born, we hired a nanny. Her name was Li.

We went down to Flushing to meet her. The agency was a storefront off Main Street: a counter, a wall of photographs of smiling babies with no names beneath them, and a row of folding chairs where three women waited with their bags on their laps. The interviews took place in a back room. The first woman praised the baby — white skin, like her mother's, big eyes, double lids. Li was the second. She was small and straight, somewhere in her fifties, in a gray coat shiny at the cuffs. She sat across from my wife, placed her hands on her knees, and kept them there. My wife did the interview while I held the baby and understood that I was to say nothing. Li answered questions as if signing documents: briefly, completely, looking at the paper rather than the clerk. She had cared for two infants, both in Queens. She kept a room in Flushing. Underground, she said, and waited for the next question. She could start Monday.

Four hundred a week. We had just closed on the house in Irvington, at a number I had reached by a method best described as faith, and four hundred a week was, by any honest reading of our accounts, more than we had. I did the division in my head at that table — per month, per year, against the mortgage — while my wife and Li settled it in two sentences, and I said nothing, and we paid it, every Friday, in cash, in an envelope.

There was one condition, and Li stated it before my wife could reach the question. She worked Monday through Saturday. Saturday after dinner she took the train back to Flushing; Sunday night she returned. Sunday was hers. She said it once, not unkindly, and not as the opening of a negotiation. My wife asked whether she wouldn't rather keep the room money and stay the weekends with us; the train was two hours each way. Li said no. She needed the day to herself. That was the phrase, and we took it at that, and it held for two years.

She couldn't read the station signs, so she counted stops. The agency had told her eight. She counted on her first trip up, and after that Irvington was simply the eighth stop, and she stood for the doors with her bag already on her shoulder while the seventh was still sliding past the windows. As far as we knew she had no one — no family, no friends; in all the early months she never mentioned anyone. There was the room underground in Flushing, and the eighth stop, and us.

She cooked the food of the north. She was from Tianjin — an hour from Beijing, she said — and my wife and I are both from Shanghai, rice people, so her table was not, strictly, our table: too much wheat, not enough sugar, vinegar where we wanted wine. My wife adjusted.

One Monday I woke before six to a steady patient knocking under the bedroom floor: Li, up since five, starting from flour. At eight-thirty she set the steamer on the table and lifted the lid. Baozi, and beside them a plate of huajuan, the coils tight and glossy, scallion pressed into the folds. I bit into one standing up, before the plates were even out, and under the scallion there was the old taste — the faint alkaline edge of laomian, old dough saved to raise the next batch — and I had to turn and face the window.

When I was a boy in Shanghai, wheat was holiday food: noodles, baozi, the steamed twisted rolls we called huajuan. They came a few times a year, and the day they appeared felt rescued from the calendar. My grandmother made them. She had learned in Heilongjiang, ten years, starting in the late sixties, keeping house for a son sent down to a state farm near the Soviet border, and came back to Shanghai with a starter in a jar she had kept alive through the winters there, a northerner's hands for dough, and a southerner's apologies for using them. I hadn't eaten her cooking in twenty years.

I didn't explain. Li took it as approval of the scallions, which it also was. After that the mornings had a repertoire — baozi twice a week, huajuan, sesame bing, hand-pulled noodles on Saturdays before she caught her train, the good millet porridge — and I stopped buying bagels in town, and in the first year I gained ten pounds, and my wife framed it as a compliment to Li, which it was, and which was not the whole of it, and I never said what the whole of it was.

In a drawer by the sink she kept the plastic grocery bags, folded into small tight triangles, hundreds of them, and I never learned what for.

With the baby she was neither soft nor stern; she was procedural, in the way of someone who has done a thing so many times that tenderness has been absorbed into technique. The first week, I watched her settle the baby against her shoulder with one hand while the other rested on the counter, the fingers curled — thumb against the pad of the middle finger, the last two loosely bent, the index lifted, though it no longer straightened all the way — some resting shape her hand made when it had nothing to do. I noticed it because she was new, and then she was not new anymore, and I stopped noticing.

She had opinions about us. Chiefly one, delivered at intervals, in a tone that was joking and was not joking: that our parents were a scandal. Four grandparents, four, all living, all healthy as far as she could tell from the photographs on the refrigerator — and not one of them here. In a proper family the grandmother comes when the baby comes; that is what a family is for; instead the parents stay comfortable in Shanghai and the children pay big money to an outsider. She'd say it — big money, outsider — while lifting our daughter out of the high chair, wiping her chin, carrying her off to be changed, and the words never once matched what her hands were doing, and neither of us ever dared point it out.

We had gone to Flushing for groceries — the good fish, the tofu Li trusted, winter bamboo shoots my wife had been talking about for a week — and stayed too long, and the snow the radio had promised for evening came at four. By the time we crossed the Whitestone it was coming down in curtains. On the Bronx River Parkway the road narrowed to two lanes between the trees, and the world went gray, and somewhere past Scarsdale we were driving on snow.

I felt the rear of the car let go before I understood it. The world came around us in one long smooth motion — trees crossing the windshield from left to right, a stone overpass swinging past where the road should have been, headlights where taillights belonged — and then we were still. We had turned completely around. We were facing the way we had come. In the trunk something had shifted and settled. Around us other cars sat scattered on the parkway at their own wrong angles, engines running, wipers going, nobody moving. The snow came down through everyone's high beams.

The baby began to cry.

My wife reached back to touch her foot. In the mirror Li's hand was already on the baby's leg, steadying her, the fingers curled in that resting shape. I looked back at the road, which was not going anywhere.

My wife began to sing. "My Favorite Things," from the movie everyone in our year had watched in English class. She took the melody, and my voice found the third below it without being asked — the choir had put it there, and it had stayed. We handed the lines back and forth, one phrase each, the joins clean, old habit, and in the closed car the two voices locked, and the car held the sound like a small room built for it. It was ridiculous, in a stopped car in the snow, and it was the one thing we could still make together on no notice. In the mirror the baby's face went through its weathers: the crying losing conviction, then suspicion, then the wet sudden laugh. A county plow went up the southbound side with its yellow lights turning, and the light swept through the car three times, and we kept singing.

We were starting the second verse when my wife stopped.

"Are you crying?"

I looked in the mirror. Li was sitting very straight beside the car seat, and her face was wet. She didn't turn away and she didn't wipe it. She said she was fine. Then she said she hadn't sung in a long time. My wife asked, gently, sung what — and Li was quiet, and then said she had sung opera. Peking opera. In Tianjin, and then other places. Before she came here.

She had been in our house four months. She had never mentioned it.

My wife asked her to sing something. Li shook her head. The baby said ah, and reached toward her. My wife asked again — really, please, look at the road, we're not going anywhere — and Li looked out the window at the stopped parkway for a while, the trees black under their loads.

Then her back began to straighten. It rose from the base like something being assembled, and the shoulder belt caught it at the collarbone and pressed it back. She paused. She reached down and unbuckled the belt.

The click was very loud. I almost said something — cars were still sliding by in the other lane, we were sideways to traffic — and then I didn't. The belt drew itself back across her chest, and she came forward off the seatback and sat on the edge of the seat, touching nothing, taller than the car was built for. She breathed in — and I watched for the lift our choir director used to demand, the ribs opening like a case. It didn't come. Her shoulders dropped. Something went down instead of up, and settled low, and her chest never moved again for four lines.

I had sung for years. I knew what a supported voice was; I knew, roughly, what one cost. The first note told me I knew nothing. It was high without being thin, enormous without being loud — a sound with a spine in it — and it did not fit in the car. The baby's hand stopped halfway to the whale rattle and hung there. My eyes went to the road, to the mirror, to the road. On the glass beside Li a bloom of fog had appeared and was widening with every phrase.

On the second line her arms began to open — some gesture starting in them, wide, meant for a bigger room — and her elbow knocked the window, a small hard sound, and the arms came back in, and the gesture finished as one slow turn of the wrist, the fingers still holding that curled shape, drawing a circle the size of a teacup. Her sleeve caught the rattle on the way down. The plastic whale went ding-ding into the middle of it. She didn't stop. Something moved down by the floor — I couldn't see what — and the baby's bottle rolled out from under the seat and knocked against the door, and the voice went over all of it, a river over stones.

She wasn't singing to us. She was singing to the window, to her own dim face in the black glass with the snow coming down behind it. In the next lane a man who had gotten out to look at his fender stood in the falling snow with his head turned toward our car, listening to whatever fraction of it reached him.

Four lines. On the last one her hands came together in front of her, palm to palm, and stayed there, and the sound moved somewhere behind the bones of her face and came out thin and shaking and held — held past the point where my own lungs, breathing along the only way they knew how, had emptied — and then it was gone, and the car rang with it, and the wipers went once across the glass.

The click again. She had put the belt back on. Her hands settled flat on her knees, all five fingers straight.

The baby made a sound I had never heard from her — a long ah on one pitch, holding it, trying it. Then she laughed and beat the car seat with both hands.

My wife had turned all the way around, her hand over her mouth. She asked what it was.

"A wife dreams her husband comes home from the war," Li said. She leaned down, picked the bottle up off the floor, and set it back in its place.

Ahead of us, taillights had begun to creep. Somebody tapped a horn, twice, not at anyone. I put the car in drive and brought us around, very slowly, until we were pointed home.

It was Monday when we learned about the son, because it was always Monday. If she had news, it appeared in the kitchen, without preface, while she cooked. That Monday she was breaking down a chicken and said, without turning around, that her son was getting married in the spring.

My wife stopped where she was. "Your son?"

"In Tianjin." The cleaver went through a joint. "The girl is from Jinan. A good family." And then, because the wedding needed a place to happen: the apartment was nearly ready, her husband was seeing to the last of it — the fixtures, the floors. He knew people who could get the materials at the right price.

So there was a husband: a man in Tianjin, seeing to things.

My wife asked the questions — how old was the son, what did he do, when in the spring — and Li answered each one at her fixed length. Thirty-four. A policeman. The eighth of April, a good date. My wife asked how long she had been away from them, and Li said the son had been sixteen when she left, and moved from the chicken to the scallions, and the kitchen filled with the smell of them.

Neither of us said anything about scandals, or about what a family is for, or about the grandmother who comes when the baby comes. Neither did she.

On the eighth of April, a Thursday, she made dinner as usual, and in the middle of the table there was a dish we hadn't seen before — meatballs the size of a fist, four of them, glazed dark, on a white plate. She didn't announce it. My wife looked at the plate, and looked at her, and said it looked like banquet food, and Li said, "Four-joy meatballs. For weddings." She served the baby first. We ate all four. At some point in that meal it was nine o'clock the next morning in Tianjin, and her son's car would have been arriving at the girl's door, and she was rinsing the plate.

The photographs came six weeks later, by mail to Flushing, and up to us on a Monday. She laid them on the kitchen table in order: the couple at the restaurant entrance under an arch of red balloons; the couple with the girl's parents; the tables — she counted them off for us, twenty-two; the son alone, in a rented gray suit a size large in the shoulders, smiling like a man who had been told to relax. He had her jaw. Then a photograph with a thin man at the edge of the group, in a dark jacket zipped to the throat, his hands at his sides, his face narrow and unsmiling. Li touched the photo once, at the margin, and said, "My husband," and squared the stack, and the photographs went back into their envelope.

We didn't ask what he did. I looked at the thin man and wondered if he had sung too, once. And under that, the other question: twenty-two tables, and the envelopes every Friday. I asked neither. She fit the envelope of photographs into the inside pocket of her bag, where it wouldn't bend, and we never saw them again.

That autumn, over the counting of the Friday envelope, she mentioned that they had bought the second apartment. My wife asked — carefully; money questions went one way in that kitchen — how they had managed the first. They had bought it in 2000, Li said, the first year it was possible. Everyone said they were crazy. Her husband said it; her son said it. An apartment was not a thing you bought — the government assigned you one through your job, and you paid a few yuan a month in rent — and now sixty thousand, to own the same rooms they already lived in? She had written home that they would pay it — all of it, the savings and a mortgage on top, and the mortgage frightened her husband more than the price. "I was here," she said, wiping the counter in long strokes. "I could see what things cost when people want them." The wedding had waited for that apartment. Now it was worth double, the rent from the second would carry the mortgage, and in the spring there would be a third — old, cheap, top floor, no elevator; her husband would fix it slowly. "Tianjin is going up," she said, and it was not a prediction.

That night I sat at my desk with the mortgage folder in front of me, not looking at it, and did her arithmetic instead. Four hundred a week, less the room, less the train, fifty-two weeks, eighteen years — against Tianjin prices, doubled since 2000 and climbing. The numbers closed like a proof: it was all going home, and it had all been placed, at the one moment placing it was possible, by the only member of the family who believed. I had bought this house the way everyone I knew bought houses — the bank agreed, the market agreed, faith with a co-signer. She had bought hers against her own husband, from a basement, sight unseen. I turned off the desk lamp and went up past the kitchen, where the dough board sat drying against the wall for the morning.

Our daughter had had words for a while by then — Mama; Ayi, which arrived indecently early and which my wife swears came first; Baba, third, a standing joke I pretended to be a better sport about than I was. But that second spring the talk came in like a tide: more, no, up, the names of foods, and then short sentences, and the Mandarin in them was Li's Mandarin, so that our daughter — both of her parents Shanghai people — said her first sentences with Tianjin corners on them, the r growled onto the ends of words where no one in either of our families had ever put it. My mother caught it over the phone. Who is teaching her to talk like that? The nanny, my wife said, and my mother went quiet. 

And there was a game the two of them had. Our daughter would plant herself in the middle of the kitchen, take a breath with her whole body, and hold a note — the long ah she had found in the snowstorm, longer now, steadier — and Li would stop whatever her hands were doing and listen to the end of it, without a word, the way you listen to a colleague. Then she would go back to the dough. No praise; or the listening was the praise. Once I saw her take the small hand afterward and fold it, briefly, into that resting shape of hers, and our daughter laughed and unmade it.

On a Monday in June, over the breakfast dishes, Li said she needed a day — a Monday, the next week. A woman she knew, a newcomer, a nanny with a family in the city, had a hearing at the immigration court downtown, and Li would cover her job for the day so the woman wouldn't lose the position. "If she misses it," Li said, rinsing a bowl, "everything is finished." She had already arranged our side of it: a friend of hers, reliable, would come up Sunday night and stay through Monday.

I asked why not send the friend downtown instead, and Li could stay with us, where she knew the child, knew the house.

Li was quiet for a moment. Then she said the newcomer worked for an American family.

"You don't speak English either," I said.

She said she would explain it to me later. Then she asked our daughter whether she wanted more egg, and was told yes, more, in both languages.

The week absorbed the question.

Sunday night we drove to the station as usual, and the woman who got off the train was not Li. She looked old at first. Later I decided it was the thinness, and the care with which she held herself. She wore a good coat, too warm for June, and a scarf pinned at the throat, and she found us on the platform before we found her. She said Li had sent her. In the car she stated her qualifications: she had raised two children and cared for others; she could take full charge of the child; she could not cook. She said the last part first — before her name, almost. My wife said it was fine, it was one day, she'd be working from home anyway. She gave us her full name — the surname was Ma — and looked out the window at the dark going by, and said the houses here had so much space between them.

Monday I left at seven. My wife set up at the dining table with the phone and her files. The morning, she told me afterward, went well enough — Ma was competent with our daughter, quick with the diaper, sure-handed in a way you could see was old knowledge. Our daughter woke from her nap in the early afternoon, and Ma said she would take her out for some air — the stroller walk Li always did. My wife was on a call. She nodded and pointed them toward the door and mouthed not too far, and heard the stroller wheels go down the ramp I'd built over the porch steps, and went back to her call.

I came home early. It was a Monday habit that summer — leave early, beat the traffic on 9A, buy back an hour of the evening. 9A along there is two lanes and people drive it like four. I was half a mile from our turn when I saw, ahead on the right, at the edge of the sidewalk where the sidewalk more or less gives up, a stroller.

Our stroller. I knew the canopy. It was standing parallel to the road, a few feet from a phone booth, close enough to the curb that the wind off the passing trucks would have been reaching into it, and there was no one beside it.

I don't remember deciding anything. I was stopped on the shoulder with the flashers on and I was out of the car and the traffic was going by me at fifty and my daughter was sitting in the stroller with her fists in her lap, watching the cars, perfectly calm, doing what I suppose she thought was the activity, with the person somewhere behind her. I had the straps off and had her up and against my shoulder — "Baba," she said, pleased with the arrangement, and pointed at a truck — and over the hood of the stroller I saw the booth door fold open, and Ma came out with a smile still on her face from somewhere else, and it stopped when it reached us.

I didn't say anything. If Ma did, I don't remember it. I had her in the car seat and the door shut and I was pulling into traffic, and in the mirror there was a woman in a good coat standing on the margin of 9A beside an empty stroller, getting smaller, and I made the turn and lost her.

My wife met me at the door already talking, the ordinary evening things, and stopped when she saw our daughter in my arms — the child, and no stroller, and no Ma. I told it in about four sentences. My wife took her from me and looked her over, her hands not quite steady, and she was fine — fine, and hungry, saying apple, reaching for the fruit bowl. My wife was at the front window when she said, "She's coming," and I looked, and up the middle of our quiet street came Ma, walking straight and unhurried, pushing the empty stroller in front of her like a cart being returned at the market.

My wife opened the door. Ma did not come past the hall. She parked the stroller against the wall with the brake on, and said the child had been within her sight the entire time, and that she understood, and that if we would allow it she would go back tonight instead of tomorrow. My wife looked at me.

I drove her. It is eleven minutes to the Irvington station and we did not speak. Both of us understood Mandarin; the silence was not a condition, it was a work, and we did it the whole way. She looked out her window. I looked at the road. Since the booth door folded open I had been thinking the same thing — there is a phone in the house — and I carried it the eleven minutes and said nothing.

At the station she got out and walked ahead of me to the door, and I went to the window and bought her ticket to Grand Central — one way, off-peak — and turned and held it out. She took it with both hands. She thanked me — formally, completely, a whole sentence with my surname in it, and a small dip of the head — and it was the only thing said between us since 9A, and there was nothing wrong with it at all. Then she went out to the southbound platform, and I waited inside by the schedule board, because leaving before the train came was not something I could make myself do, and going out to stand with her was not either. The train came in eighteen minutes. Through the window I watched her board, and watched the train go, and then I drove home.

That night, in bed, my wife told me the rest of the day. In the morning, while she'd made lunch and Ma held our daughter, they had talked — where we were from, how we'd met, how we'd come to America. My wife told it: the choir, the university, the visa years. Ma listened to all of it, holding the child, nodding along. She had asked questions too — about the towns, at one point: which ones the train stopped at, how far Dobbs Ferry was — and my wife had answered and thought nothing of it, a newcomer learning the area. And then Ma said, "You should have married an American."

My wife had laughed, not knowing what else to do, and asked why, and Ma had not laughed. She said, "It's the only way to a better life here," and jogged our daughter gently on her knee, and looked around our dining room. In the dark, my wife did the look for me: a slow circuit of the ceiling.

My wife told it in a flat voice, turning it over — an object she didn't know the use of. I stared at the ceiling and, entirely against my will, heard the arithmetic start — the mortgage, the four hundred — and stopped it. "She left the baby by the road," I said, which was an answer to nothing, and my wife said, "I know," and we went to sleep, or lay there.

Li came back Tuesday on the early train and was in the kitchen when I came down, the dough board already going. She turned it off — the work, I mean; she stopped, dried her hands, and faced me, which she did not ordinarily do, and apologized. It was not elaborate. She said the fault was hers: she had chosen the person, and the choosing had been wrong, and it would not happen again — no more weekdays off; if there was another hearing, another newcomer, someone else could do the covering. She said it once, completely, like terms. I said our daughter was fine. She nodded and went back to the dough.

She did not explain why Ma could not have gone to the American family, and I did not ask again.

A few weeks later, on an evening in July, the doorbell rang while we were at dinner, and I opened the door to two policemen, and between them, Ma.

She was in the good coat, in the heat, the scarf pinned at the throat. She did not look at me. She stood between the officers the way you stand for a photograph you didn't ask for.

The younger officer was Chinese-American, and after the older one had established who I was, the younger did the talking. There had been a call, he said, from a resident in Dobbs Ferry. This woman had been seen in his neighborhood repeatedly over some weeks — on the street, outside a place of business, once in the evening in the yard. Today she was there again and the resident called, and they picked her up. She carried no identification. She gave them an address in Flushing where she said she lived, with a woman named Li — but the woman named Li, she told them, worked upstate as a nanny and did not come home in the week, so there was no one there to say so. Then she produced a card with this address on it, in my wife's handwriting.

The question, the officer said, was whether anyone could account for her. Otherwise — and here he was careful — otherwise there were places; the county could take care of her.

So it was not about us. Not the envelopes, not the cash, not the two years of Fridays; not the papers she did not have, which we had known from the first afternoon. None of it was on the table tonight — only that I did not, in any sense that would satisfy the man in Dobbs Ferry, know this woman at all.

The water in the kitchen had stopped; I did not know when. Li was in the hall, a towel motionless in her hands, looking at the uniforms. One full second nobody in that hallway breathed, and I understood what a police uniform was to her. I had managed not to for two years. Then she finished drying her hands, hung the towel over her shoulder — the work not done — and walked past me to the door.

Yes, she said. She knew her. They were roommates. The Flushing address was right; Ma lived with her; she would answer for her.

The younger officer translated the older one's question. Was she certain — because if not, they or the county could take the woman off everyone's hands; it could all be taken care of. And Ma, standing between them, heard all of it. I watched her hear the words taken care of in her own language, looking at the middle distance, her chin level.

"I'm certain," Li said.

There was paperwork of some minor kind, done on the hood of the cruiser in the last of the light. Then the policemen left, and the three of us stood in the hallway, and nobody was speechless — it was simpler than that; there was nothing that could be said in front of everyone that would not make something worse. Li went to her bag on the hook by the kitchen and came back working a key off her own ring, and pressed it into Ma's hand and closed the hand around it. Then she said she would walk her to the train.

From the front window I watched them go down our street in the dusk, the two of them, not talking as far as I could tell, Ma very straight in the good coat, Li half a step behind her, not guarding her, not leaving her.

"Go," my wife said, without turning from the window.

I got the keys and caught up with them two blocks on. Li came to my window. She thanked me and said they would walk; the trains ran late on that line and there was no hurry.

Li came back forty minutes later and put the kettle on. While it heated she told us, unasked, what she knew.

She had found Ma in the spring, on Main Street. Not homeless — Ma was well dressed, that day too; she was standing outside the bakery at ten in the morning with a suitcase, reading the bus schedules taped inside the window, and something about how long she stood there. They talked. Ma had a daughter here, married to an American, a beautiful house upstate somewhere — and there had been some difficulty; it was not the American tradition, Ma said, for the mother-in-law to live with the couple. So she had left. Or she had been asked to leave. Or she had wanted to make an independent living — Li gave us all three, in a row, without choosing among them. Ma had promised half the rent. Li had a bed free behind the curtain's other side. That was the whole of the arrangement.

Ma worked some hours in a grocery on Roosevelt, and went to school — the free English classes at the library in Queens, for immigrants. There was a man there, Li said — an older man, an Arab; "in her eyes," Li said, without inflection, "a white man." He didn't work; he lived up in one of these river towns, with his son's family. Ma said he was in love with her. He wanted to marry her. Li had asked her once, practically, when she would move in with him, and Ma had said they were still negotiating terms.

Li had not known about the neighborhood. The wandering. She said this and then said nothing after it, and the kettle came up to a boil and she turned it off and didn't pour.

I asked why she kept her. Half the rent — was it even coming?

Li looked at the table. Then she told me about the daughter, which was not an answer. Ma had spent everything on that girl. Everything, for years: the schools, the clothes, and finally the sending — she had sent the daughter to America to marry an American; that had always been the plan, Ma's plan, and it had worked. The daughter married; there was the beautiful house. Ma wanted to visit. The daughter said the time wasn't right. She said it again the next year. Then one summer Ma joined a tour group, ten days, and on the eighth day she left it and took a bus and knocked on the door of the beautiful house. She saw the house. Li paused there, and did not describe the visit; she had never been given it to describe. And then Ma was not on the tour anymore, and not in the house, and was in Flushing reading bus schedules with a suitcase.

Li stood, and poured the water out of the kettle into the sink, unused, and said good night, and went up.

On a Sunday night at the end of August, Li came up the platform stairs with her bag on her shoulder and a large paper sack, its handles taped. In the car she said it was for us — from the newcomer, the one she had covered for in June; the green card had come through. The sack rode on my wife's lap the whole way, smelling of star anise, and at home we unpacked it on the counter: dried mushrooms, two boxes of tea, a tin of the good haw flakes, and a small padded jacket for our daughter, red, embroidered, a size too big.

Our daughter claimed it off the counter before anyone had ruled on it — mine — and Li put her into it, and knelt, and rolled each cuff twice, with a seamstress's economy, and turned her by the shoulders to face us. The jacket came to her knees. "By the new year it fits," Li said. Our daughter looked down at herself and said piaoliang — pretty — with the northern r on it, and Li said something back too low for me to catch, in the voice she kept for that altitude.

"She's legal now," Li said, folding the sack along its creases. "She can go home and come back. She can do anything." She said it gladly, and from across a fence.

I asked whether Ma had a green card.

Li was quiet for a moment. Ma was not getting one, she said; Ma's plan was the marriage. By that Monday in June she had already known Ma had an interest in white men. "Married, not married," Li said. "It doesn't matter to her." She could not send that into a stranger's house and have it be her doing. So she had sent Ma to us, where the risk was — and she did not finish the sentence.

The kitchen was quiet except for our daughter's monitor ticking with her breath. It was the second professional opinion of the summer. Ma had offered the first, in July, to my wife.

I asked Li whether she wanted one — a green card.

"No," Li said.

I asked why not.

Ma's way was closed to her, she said: she had a husband. That left the newcomer's way. You said you were persecuted. You made the evidence. There were people who arranged it; you went and stood in front of the consulate on Twelfth Avenue with signs, on the right days, and someone photographed you, and the photographs went in the file. The papers were not true; the hearing was real. She knew exactly how it was done. She recited the steps like a recipe she had decided against. "I'm not ready to do that," she said — as if it were a dish requiring more nerve than she currently kept in the house.

"And for what," she said. "My family is in Tianjin."

Then, over the tea she finally poured, the whole arithmetic, most of which we knew by then and had never heard assembled: the son, the wife from Jinan, the grandson coming in the winter — a first grandchild, who would come into the world while she was here, spooning egg into the mouth of another family's child; the three apartments in a city going up; eighteen years, and the goal now close enough to count in seasons rather than years. She had one chance, she said — that was the shape of her whole situation, one chance: the day she flew home, the door closed behind her. No visits in between, ever; she had known that since 1986 and had spent eighteen years not testing it.

I asked how she had come, in '86.

The troupe, she said. They had been performing in Kentucky — a tour, arranged high up, a big thing at the time. And at the end of it the troupe did not go home. All of them — she moved her hand flatly over the table — everyone stayed. The others were still there, in Kentucky, as far as she knew; there had been work, and then families, and then it was eighteen years. She was the only one who left for New York.

I asked why New York.

"Because the music is here," she said. "The opera. Lincoln Center." She handled the English words like borrowed tools. "I thought, if I am going to be in America, I will be where it is."

I asked — knowing the answer somewhere in my body before I finished the question — how many she had seen. Shows. In eighteen years.

Li turned her cup a quarter turn on the table.

"The tickets are expensive," she said.

When she went home, she said — when the money was done — she would not fly straight to Tianjin. She would fly the other way first. East. To Paris. She knew it was the wrong direction; she said it before I could — "the wrong way around the world," she said, as if quoting the objection of everyone she'd ever told. There was an opera house there; she had seen pictures of the staircase. She would buy one ticket, a real seat, hear one opera in that house — it did not matter which one — and the next day begin the long flight home from the wrong side of the earth. One evening. She had been deciding it for years, she said, as she decided everything, and it was decided.

Then she stood, and rinsed her cup, and said the practical coda, drying her hands: when the time came — and it was coming, a year, maybe less — she would tell us long in advance. Months. Enough to find someone good, enough for the child to get used to the idea. "No surprise," she said, in English, both words, and hung the towel on its bar, and went up to bed.

Sunday I waited at the Irvington station. Li was always among the first three off the train — she liked the car near the front, she'd told my wife once, because it stopped closest to the stairs. The first three were a man with a garment bag, a teenage girl, a man with a garment bag. Then everyone. Then no one, and the train pulled out, and the platform lights stood in their cones of cold.

We called her phone that night, and Monday morning, and Monday night. She had owned it four months; my wife had programmed our number into it herself, under A, so it would be first. It rang and rang and did not go anywhere. There was no voicemail. Li had never wanted to record the greeting.

Tuesday I called the agency in Flushing that had first sent her to us. A woman took down my number and said she would ask. She did not call back.

Wednesday night, after my wife and the baby were asleep, I sat down at the computer and typed Li's name into the search field, and looked at what I had typed, and stopped.

I had a surname. Li — 李; no mystery in that, it was on every envelope in my wife's hand: 李姐, Big Sister Li. That was what we called her. Our daughter called her Ayi — auntie — no surname in it at all. Between the two names we had the whole of it: the most common surname in China, an older sister, an aunt. What I did not have was the rest. In two years I had never heard her full name said, and she had signed nothing in front of me. I went and got the folder where we kept the house papers. The agency's receipt was there from two years ago — the fee, the date, cash — and no name on it anywhere but ours.

I searched for what I remembered instead.

She had sung four lines in the car, two winters ago, and I had understood them in pieces — caught at speed, kept without being asked. A broken mirror, made round. A golden seal. I typed broken mirror golden seal Peking opera in Chinese, and the second result was the aria.

Spring Chamber Dream. Cheng Yanqiu, 1931\. I read the synopsis at the top of the page. A wife waits at home for a husband taken by the war. One afternoon she dozes at her table, and he comes through the door, and she weeps and scolds him and forgives him and they sit together as the evening comes on. The audience knows from the first scene what she does not: he has already been killed. The whole reunion happens inside her dream, and she is the only one in the theater who doesn't know she is dreaming it.

Below the synopsis was the text. I found the four lines partway down the page:

*你回来也算是重圆破镜，*   
*休再要觅封侯辜负香衾。*   
*粗茶饭还胜那黄金斗印，*   
*愿此生常相守怜我怜卿。*

*You've come home — the broken mirror is whole again.*   
*Seek no more rank and fame to slight our shared quilt.*   
*Plain tea and coarse rice outweigh the golden seal of office.*   
*Let us keep each other all this life — you pitying me, I pitying you.*

I read them, and read them again, and sat in the small light of the screen with the house quiet above me.

On another page, hours in by then, there was a photograph of a performer's hand — the thumb against the pad of the middle finger, the last two fingers bent, the index lifted. The caption gave the shape a name. Orchid fingers. It said students hold their hands that way for years before they are allowed to do anything else. Like this. Upstairs my daughter was asleep with her hands flung open, and I sat in the light of the screen for a long time.

The agency called back Thursday, at last, with an address. Friday after work we put our daughter in the car and drove to Flushing — the same road as the grocery runs, run backward, the parkway bare and gray, no weather at all.

The house was on a side street off Kissena, one of those houses that had been divided and divided again — three mailboxes by the front door, a fourth taped to the railing, a row of shoes inside the storm door that belonged to more people than any one family. Li's entrance was around the side, down five concrete steps past a drain cover, to a metal door painted the color of the brick. My wife carried our daughter. The stairwell smelled of other people's dinners. I knocked.

Ma opened the door on the chain first, then all the way.

She was dressed as she was always dressed, as if for an appointment that might yet be kept — the good coat, though she was indoors, a scarf pinned at the throat. She looked at us, and at the child, and said, "Li Jie's family." Then she stepped back and held the door, and it would have been an insult not to go in.

The apartment was one room and a corner of a room. A curtain on a wire divided it. On the near side there was a two-ring burner, a small table with a folding chair and a stool, Ma's things — a radio, a stack of magazines, a mirror hung too high on a nail. She put water on before we had refused anything and set out two cups and then, after a look at my wife, a third for herself, and we sat, my wife on the chair with our daughter on her lap, I on the stool, Ma on the edge of a bed I understood was hers.

The curtain was partway open. On the other side was Li's half: a single bed made so tight the blanket shone, a folded quilt squared at its foot, a wall calendar from a Flushing supermarket still on the old month. On the floor, a clean rectangle in the thin dust where a suitcase had stood. Nothing else. I had known, in the way you know things you've been told, that she sent everything home. I had not known it looked like this.

Ma poured the tea. Then she told us, in order: a message had come from home, Sunday before dawn — the husband gravely ill, come at once. Li had packed within the hour. Ma had walked her to Main Street; Li had taken the bus to the airport rather than wait for anything better. That was Sunday. There had been nothing since. It had the sound of an account already given more than once.

"I hope her husband recovers," my wife said. "Maybe then she'll call us."

Ma held her cup with both hands and looked into it.

"No," she said.

She let it sit there, and for a moment I thought she meant Li would not call. Then she said that on Sunday night the telephone had rung — someone from home, asking for Li. Ma told them Li had already left for the airport. There was a long pause on the line, Ma said. Long enough that she asked if they were still there.

Then the caller told her: the husband had died a week ago. They hadn't put it in the message. They didn't want to make her too sad, the caller said. And they wanted her home quickly.

Nobody said anything. Above us, through the ceiling, a television changed channels. Our daughter, gone quiet in the strange room, had gotten hold of my wife's collar and was working it, and that small industry was the only sound among us.

I heard myself ask what would happen with the apartment.

Ma set her cup down. Her daughter would be moving in, she said. Maybe next week.

"Your daughter," I said.

"Didn't Li tell you?"

"Li said she married an American. A big house."

Ma was quiet for a moment. Her hand went to the pin at her throat and straightened it, though it had not moved.

"No," she said. "She was trying to. There were boyfriends. They paid her — not big money. She doesn't speak English." She looked at the curtain, at Li's half of the room. "She goes out every night. But she has agreed to come live with me. Maybe next week."

She turned her cup a little on the table.

"Four years in this room," she said. "She bought nothing. One coat — the same coat. Saturday nights she came in on the late train, and Sunday — you know what her Sundays were? Nine o'clock, the remittance office on Main Street, before the line. Every Sunday, rain, snow. Whatever was in the envelope, minus the rent, minus the train. Then she came back here and mended, and slept a few hours, and Sunday night she was on the train again, going back to you." She straightened the pin at her throat again. "I told her once — keep one week. One week for yourself. Spend something. She said, in Tianjin it's worth three times as much."

Our daughter, bored with the collar, turned in my wife's arms and surveyed the room, and said, to no one in it, "Ayi." She said it again, to the curtain. "Ayi lai" — Ayi, come.

Ma looked at her.

"You know, I never heard her sing," she said. "Four years, one room. Nothing — not humming, not with the radio. Then one Sunday last spring she comes back and tells me the little girl in Irvington can hold a note. I said, how would you know a held note. She didn't answer me." Ma picked up her cup and set it down again without drinking. "So. She sang for you."

Nobody had told her that. My wife looked at me. Neither of us corrected her.

We finished the tea because leaving it would have been worse. At the door, while my wife was working our daughter's arms back into her coat, Ma went to the table and came back with something in her fist and held it out to me. Our house keys, on the ring my wife had given her, with the green tag from the hardware store still on it. She had left them, Ma said. On the table. Before the airport.

We climbed the five steps. The metal door shut behind us before we reached the sidewalk.

On the Van Wyck the traffic barely moved. Our daughter asked for her Ayi twice, and then gave it up, and said, "Mama, sing." My wife sang. I came in on the second line. In the mirror our daughter's face went through its weathers and settled somewhere short of a laugh.

I wished there was another snowstorm.

We were almost at the bridge when my wife stopped singing. She was looking out her window at the river of taillights, and she asked:

"If she'd found out before boarding — that he was already gone — do you think she would have changed her flight? To Paris, to see the opera?"
